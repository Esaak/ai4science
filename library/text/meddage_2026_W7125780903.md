ABSTRACT
Machine learning techniques have become increasingly prevalent in wind engineering applica-
tions for tall buildings. However, most existing studies have focused on predicting either static 
pressure or time-dependent pressure on a single tall building. In this study, a machine learning- 
based surrogate model was developed to predict wind pressure time histories on rectangular tall 
buildings with varying slenderness and side ratios under different wind directions. A series of 
wind tunnel experiments was conducted on six rectangular tall buildings at a scale of 1:300 in a 
boundary layer wind tunnel. Validated computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models, integrating 
embedded large eddy simulations for turbulence modelling, were used to generate wind pressure 
time histories for 15 rectangular tall buildings with varying geometric proportions under seven 
wind directions (0◦, 15◦, 30◦, 45◦, 60◦, 75◦, and 90◦). These CFD data were subsequently used to 
train a surrogate model based on an attention-based neural network. The neural network sur-
rogate model accurately captured the time-dependent wind pressure distributions over the sur-
faces of tall buildings with different geometries, achieving an R2 value of 0.98. It was able to 
closely replicate and reconstruct complex flow features such as separation, conical vortices, and 
steep pressure gradients, in good agreement with CFD results. Additionally, statistical measures of 
pressure coefficients, mean, standard deviation, and extreme values predicted by the neural 
network showed good agreement with corresponding (unseen) data from wind tunnel experi-
ments. The proposed surrogate model is a promising complementary approach for accurately 
predicting unsteady wind pressure on tall buildings with varying geometries within a few 
seconds.

unsteady wind loading on tall buildings has been a subject of extensive research in recent decades.
Experimental investigations into unsteady wind loading on tall buildings were initiated through wind tunnel modelling [4–6]. 
Wind tunnel experiments have been conducted for no less than 50 years and are widely regarded as a reliable method within the wind 
engineering community, despite requiring specialised expertise, substantial time, physical effort, and high costs [7]. For design 
purposes, wind loading guidelines for tall buildings have been codified based on previous wind tunnel experiments. However, wind 
loading standards consider only the static pressure distribution on the building envelope, which differs significantly from unsteady 
wind pressures. Depending on the flow characteristics, intense negative pressures may develop on building surfaces, resulting in high 
suction loads on cladding. Furthermore, wind loading standards such as AS/NZS 1170.2:2021 [8] do not cover buildings exceeding 
200 m in height, necessitating wind tunnel testing for such cases. To assess unsteady wind pressures on tall buildings with high 
slenderness ratios, design engineers must rely on wind tunnel experiments, although using this approach in regular basis is often 
impractical. As a result, numerical modelling techniques, such as Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) have been developed and 
validated based on wind tunnel data to simulate wind pressure acting on tall buildings [9,10].
Among pioneering researchers, Blocken [9] suggested that CFD modelling can provide more comprehensive information on the 
flow characteristics around tall buildings across the entire domain. CFD modelling can be applied to a range of configurations: from a 
simple isolated-building scenario to complex urban settings, to investigate wind flow. Compared to the physical effort required in wind 
tunnel experiments (e.g., model fabrication and tubing), CFD offers a more convenient and flexible means of modifying geometries 
within the computational domain. Nevertheless, the accuracy and the simulation time of CFD strongly depend on the computational 
grid (mesh arrangement) [3] which influences how turbulence is modelled or resolved in the domain.
Turbulence modelling in CFD simulations can be categorised into scale-resolving techniques, such as Large Eddy Simulation (LES), 
and modelling-based approaches, such as the Reynolds-Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) method. RANS modelling has been widely 
adopted in wind engineering due to its ability to simulate high Reynolds number flows with relatively low computational cost [3]. In 
RANS, the effects of turbulence are implicitly modelled by averaging the Navier-Stokes equations [11], without resolving individual 
eddies. Instead, LES explicitly resolves the larger, energy-containing turbulent eddies, while the smaller eddies are modelled using 
subgrid-scale (SGS) models [3]. Liu and Niu [12] reported that wind pressure obtained through Large Eddy Simulation (LES) is closer 
to wind tunnel results than that obtained using the RANS approach. Nevertheless, LES requires a refined mesh, especially near wall 
surfaces, resulting in higher computational demands compared to RANS. Using LES regularly for structural design or wind load cal-
culations of tall buildings is challenging, as the results cannot be obtained within a short time frame. For this reason, complementary 
approaches are required that can accurately and quickly predict unsteady wind pressure on tall buildings.
Machine learning is one such complementary method, capable of learning from data without being explicitly programmed. It has 
recently been widely applied in structural engineering, including wind engineering research [13]. In the context of tall buildings, 
machine learning can be used to learn the relationships among geometric parameters, wind direction, and time-dependent wind 
pressure. Compared to wind tunnel testing or numerical simulations, a properly trained machine learning model can provide pre-
dictions for new input data in just a few seconds [14]. This makes it a suitable complementary approach for predicting wind loads on 
buildings.
Previous researchers have used data-driven machine learning in estimating wind loads on tall buildings. Hu et al. [15] developed 
deep learning and classical machine learning models to predict wind pressure on a tall building interfered with by a nearby tall 
building. Among the models, Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) achieved the highest R2 scores: 0.988 for mean pressure pre-
dictions and 0.924 for fluctuating pressure predictions. Wang et al. [16] proposed a machine learning approach using the same dataset 
and their extreme gradient boosting model obtained R2 of 0.99 for training and testing predictions of wind pressure. Glumac et al. [17] 
used machine learning methods to predict static mean and fluctuating pressure coefficients on a tall building and recommended neural 
network models as the best-performing approach for pressure coefficient prediction. Dongmei et al. [18] and Nav et al. [19] combined 
neural networks with Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) to predict wind pressures on a tall building. An unsupervised learning 
method was used by Kim et al. [20] and Wang and Shu [21] to cluster the wind pressure characteristics on tall buildings, reporting that 
it can reveal hidden patterns in complex wind pressure fields. Out of these works, the majority of studies focused on predicting static 
wind pressure quantities on tall buildings using machine learning methods [22–26].
Modelling time-dependent/unsteady wind pressure on tall buildings became increasingly important as it can estimate the peak 
wind pressure on cladding which differs significantly from time-averaged pressure. Huang et al. [27] developed a deep neural network 
with Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO) to predict missing wind pressure data in a time history. They selected the supertall building 
Ping-An Finance Centre (600 m) to collect wind measurements. The model demonstrated good accuracy in recovering the missing 
pressure data. Hu et al. [28] developed a deep learning model to predict the cross-wind base spectra of tall buildings using the data 
obtained through experimental and numerical modelling. Li et al. [29] used classical machine learning and neural network models to 
predict the wind loading power spectrum on tall buildings, using the Commonwealth Advisory Aeronautical Research Council 
(CAARC) building as a case study. Their models achieved a strong correlation of 0.97 between the predicted and observed values. 
Related to pressure on building envelope, Chen et al. [30] developed a time series of wind predictions on a tall building using deep 
learning methods and their wavelet neural network obtained the best predictive performance (correlation between 0.93 and 0.99). A 
generative deep learning approach was used by Liu et al. [31] to predict the time-dependent pressure on a tall building using the data 
from a sparse sensor network. Recently, these modelling approaches have been improved by including spatial coordinates of the 
building and multiple wind directions [7,14].

approach that accounts for geometric variability in data-driven models. When these geometries change, the wind flow characteristics

In this study, an attention-based neural network was developed to predict time-dependent wind pressure on rectangular tall 
buildings. Compared with state-of-the-art generative models such as generative adversarial networks (GAN)s, the proposed archi-
tecture is considerably easier to optimise, as it avoids adversarial training. Furthermore, GANs are primarily designed for structured 
image-like data and become increasingly difficult to train as the dataset size grows, whereas the present dataset consists of millions of 
tabular data. Unlike proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)-based neural networks, the proposed approach does not rely on 
dimensionality reduction, thereby retaining the full temporal information. For the dataset, different slenderness ratios (6, 8, 10) and 
side ratios (1, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2), under multiple wind attack angles (0◦, 15◦, 30◦, 45◦, 60◦, 75◦, and 90◦) were considered which resulted 
in a large and comprehensive data-driven surrogate model.
The surrogate model was developed using the data obtained from a series of CFD simulations, which were validated against a wind 
tunnel testing program. The surrogate model was trained and validated using CFD data and tested using unseen CFD-generated data. 
This model was developed using a dataset that spans a wide range of the most probable geometric dimensions of rectangular tall 
buildings and multiple wind directions. In contrast, most previous studies focused on a single building geometry and a limited set of 
wind angles, making it difficult to develop surrogate models with broader applicability. By training on diverse geometries and wind 
directions, the proposed model can reasonably interpolate between cases and provide predictions of unsteady wind pressures for 
intermediate rectangular building configurations and flow directions. Furthermore, the surrogate model's predictions were compared 
with wind tunnel measurements for additional validation. By combining all three generational approaches: wind tunnel testing, CFD 
simulations, and machine learning, this study demonstrates that machine learning methods do not replace traditional wind tunnel or 
CFD techniques. Instead, they offer a fast and accurate complementary approach for structural engineers to predict time histories of 
wind pressure on tall buildings with common rectangular geometries under multiple wind directions.

3. Numerical simulations
3.1. Numerical model development
In this study, the Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) modelling was conducted using ANSYS FLUENT 2023 [38] commercial 
software. The overall domain setup and the mesh configuration are presented in Fig. 2 where the domain size was constructed based on 
the building height (H). The upstream fetch of the domain had a 3H separation to the inlet and similar distances were maintained both 
in lateral and vertical directions. The downstream fetch was configured with a 10H distance. These separation distances have been 
used in a series of validation studies by the authors and have resulted in good agreement with wind tunnel data [1,34–37]. Polyhedral 
mesh elements were used for all the models due to their superior performance over tetrahedral and hexahedral grids in terms of 
simulation time and accuracy [1,35]. Finer elements were applied near the building, where 12 inflation layers at a growth ratio of 1.1 
were used for the building surface to capture wall-bound flows. The dimensionless wall distance (y+) was limited to a maximum of 5, 
ensuring that the first cell height is within the viscous sublayer to accurately capture near-wall flow behaviour as reported in authors’ 
previous work [1,3,36].
3.2. Flow and turbulence modelling
Turbulence was modelled using the Embedded Large Eddy Simulation (ELES) which enables the strategic use of LES and RANS 
regions within the same computational domain. In general, the LES region is confined to the zone of interest which in this case is the 
building and its surroundings, whilst RANS is applied in regions further away from the zone of interest. Adopting an ELES-based 
approach has been proven to reduce computational cost up to 50 % whilst still retaining the same numerical fidelity similar to a 
full LES simulation as comprehensively investigated in authors' previous work [7].
Inflow characteristics for the simulation were defined via the mean velocity profile U(z), turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) and 
specific dissipation rate (SDR) profiles at the inlet. Equations for these profiles and those parameters have been described in the 
author's previous work in a detailed manner [1,34–37]. Using these profiles at the inlet, the corresponding turbulence intensity 
observed in the empty fluid domain (without the building) was compared with the measured turbulence intensity profile obtained from 
the wind tunnel experiment. First, the empty domain was initialised using steady state RANS simulations with k-ω turbulence model 
until convergence (10  6) is achieved. Afterwards, the region (building to be placed) is switched to LES and transient simulation is 
carried out for 15 s while recording the velocity time histories using the monitor points applied in vertical direction. First 5 s are 
discarded in the records as the flow needs to stabilise. Inflow quantities of; (1) velocity profile, (2) turbulence intensity and (3) power 
spectral density of the flow (at a height of 0.6m) were measured in the building location of the empty domain as shown in Fig. 3. In 
Fig. 3b, L refers to the integral length scale which was 0.3 m. A strong correlation between the numerical and experimental velocity and 
turbulence intensity profiles is observed for Terrain Category III (TCIII) as per the Australian Standard [8]. The divergence between the 
results for reduced frequencies greater than 6 was mainly caused by the filtration of small-scale eddies in the mesh which was inherent.

4. Data driven surrogate modelling
4.1. Data preprocessing
The instantaneous pressure coefficient (Cp(t)) data along with geometric parameters and wind direction at each time step were 
recorded and saved into a data frame using Python programming. Fifteen different buildings, seven different wind directions, each 
with 319 monitoring points sampled at 2 kHz for 10 s resulted in approximately 670 million data points. The inputs, outputs and 
summary statistics are shown in Table 2.
The mid-point at the ground level for each building was considered as the origin from which the x, y, and z distances are calculated. 
As all the buildings are symmetric around X-axis and Z-axis, the mean value for x and z is zero. The instantaneous pressure coefficient, 
Cp(t), varies between   10.6 and + 4.6 with a relatively skewed distribution, denoted by 0.6. Surface refers to the face of the building, 
which was converted to a numerical integer; however, it was not included as an input as x, y, and z can already identify the surface of 
the building.
The final dataset was separated into three sets based on the geometries as shown in Fig. 5. Blue colour models were used for model 
training and validation using a 70/30 split. Data obtained from red colour models (B3, B10, B13 from Table 1) were used as unseen 
(test set) to evaluate the trained and validated surrogate model. It is noteworthy that the authors have covered a wide range of ge-
ometries representative of existing tall buildings, which allows the surrogate model to interpolate unsteady wind pressures for in-
termediate geometric configurations. However, like most data-driven models, its applicability is limited to the range of geometric 
ratios included in the training dataset, and extrapolation to configurations outside this range cannot be guaranteed. Among the 
variables presented in Table 2, inputs were normalised using the MinMaxScaler from Sci-Kit-Learn library [39]. It squashes the inputs 
ranging in different dimensions to a similar range which helps the machine learning surrogate model to adjust the weights and biases 
more conveniently.
4.2. Algorithm for modelling
Recent wind engineering studies have applied both classical machine-learning methods (notably tree-based ensembles) and neural 
networks. However, tree-based models can plateau in accuracy as data scale and dimensionality grow [7], because they learn via 
binary splits that can miss higher-order interactions of variables. At the same time, traditional neural networks come with their own 
drawbacks on large tabular data: they are data-intensive and tuning-sensitive. In addition, it may take significant effort to properly 
optimise a neural network for a large dataset.
To address these gaps, TabNet, a neural architecture built for tabular data, was adopted. It has a unique attention-based mechanism 
which selects the important features to acquire knowledge and patterns in the dataset [40]. TabNet model has been used in a limited 
number of research studies so far. However, it has demonstrated better performance than tree-based models, other classical machine 
learning models and traditional neural networks [41,42]. This sequential attention step allows the model to select different subsets of 
features at each stage, which capture hierarchical and conditional relationships more effectively than tree-based models. Unlike 
standard feed-forward neural networks that process all features simultaneously, TabNet focus on the most relevant features, which can 
avoid overfitting. The architecture of the TabNet Model is shown in Fig. 6.
The TabNet architecture is composed of a sequence of decision steps, each designed to selectively process and learn from different 
subsets of input features. In each step, the normalised input variables are first passed through a feature-transformer block, which stacks 
Gated Linear Unit (GLU) activations with normalisation layers to build different representations of the features while controlling 
information flow. The ‘Split’ operation divides the output of this block into two parts: one part (the decision branch) contributes 
directly to the step's intermediate prediction, while the other part (the attention branch) feeds forward to guide feature selection in the 
next step.

4.3. Optimisation of TabNet model
Given the large dataset size (~670 million instances), exhaustive hyperparameter tuning using grid search was computationally 
impractical. Therefore, a more time-efficient approach using the Optuna library in Python was adopted, which utilises advanced 
sampling strategies for efficient neural network optimisation [44]. Methods like grid search are deterministic and exhaustive which 
evaluate all combinations in a predefined hyperparameter (model settings) space. Therefore, it becomes highly computationally 
expensive for larger datasets with multiple dimensions and complex algorithms. In contrast, Optuna adopts an adaptive, form of 
Bayesian optimisation that dynamically selects hyperparameters based on past performance. Furthermore, Optuna supports early 
stopping (pruning) of underperforming trials, significantly reducing training time and computational expense. From the training data 
(approximately 375 million), 10 % of the representative data for the Optuna-based optimisation was used. The subsampling procedure 
was repeated ten times using different random seeds, and independent hyperparameter optimisation was conducted for each case. The 
resulting optimal hyperparameters were compared and the parameters yielded highest R2 scores for training and validation and

spatial location of the pressure taps. For the front surface, when the wind angle increases from 0◦ (low) to 90◦ (high), the mean pressure

surface and elevated building-induced turbulence, resulting in higher Cp,std. This effect is reversed for the right wall, which pro-

because the extreme values of the x, y, and z coordinates correspond to the edges of each surface. These edge regions are associated 
with strong building-induced turbulence and intense flow separation, producing large negative pressures and high fluctuating pres-
sures. These explanations are consistent with the observations reported in the authors’ previous work [45].

simulation typically takes between 94 and 120 clock hours to complete, depending on the dimensions of the model. In contrast, once 
the TabNet model has been trained and validated, it can predict the time histories of wind pressure for a new building within the 
bounds of the input geometries and aspect ratios in less than 20 s. This study encompasses a comprehensive range of geometries for 
rectangular tall buildings commonly found worldwide, as well as wind directions from 0◦ to 90◦. Therefore, most new buildings are 
expected to fall within the defined input parameter space. As shown in Table 4, the TabNet model offers a highly time-efficient 
complementary approach to conventional numerical simulations for predicting wind pressure. The analysis demonstrates that Tab-
Net can significantly accelerate unsteady wind pressure predictions for tall buildings. From a structural engineering perspective, wind 
design of buildings over 200 m height (B6–B15) is not covered by the Australian Wind Loading Standard [8].

5.3.2. Comparison of peak pressure coefficients extracted from time histories
The comparison of peak pressure coefficients among wind tunnel time histories and machine learning predictions has not been 
previously done, to the best of the authors' knowledge. As this is a pioneering study used machine learning to predict the time- 
dependent wind pressure on rectangular tall buildings with different geometries, the distribution of peak pressure was investigated 
at several locations of the buildings at a selected wind direction. Previous research in wind engineering has investigated various 
methods, including single worst peak, peak factor method, ensemble average, and probability distribution based on generalised 
extreme theory [46–50].

As there is only a single pressure record per pressure tap at a certain wind direction, methods like ensemble averages cannot be used 
to determine the extreme pressure values. Also, methods like peak factor would be successful only if the correct peak factor is known. 
On the other hand, some of the methods lack a strong probabilistic base. To obtain a probability distribution of peak event from a single 
time history, Quan et al. [51] introduced a new approach. This method does not rely on traditional averaging times. Instead, it is based 
on the autocorrelation of the pressure signal to identify statistically independent peak events, ensuring that each extracted peak 
represents an independent extreme event suitable for probability distribution analysis. This method does not require calculating the 
wind loads on the cladding. Instead, it focuses on investigating the statistics of peak events by comparing the pressure time histories 
from wind tunnel measurements with those predicted by TabNet.
The method splits the single-time history into several bins based on the autocorrelation function (bin size > first zero-crossing time 
of the autocorrelation) of the pressure and extracts the peak pressure of each bin to fit a probability distribution. They reported that the 
peak wind pressure near the edges and corners of the buildings follows probability distributions like generalised extreme value (GEV). 
Kasperski [52] verified that the peak pressure on the building envelope generally complies with GEV type I and III distributions. A GEV

6. Conclusions
This study includes extensive wind tunnel experiments, numerical modelling, and data-driven surrogate modelling to predict the 
unsteady wind pressure on rectangular tall buildings with varying geometric proportions under multiple wind directions. The nu-
merical modelling, in the form of CFD, was validated using wind tunnel data. Time series of wind pressure data were generated for 15 
tall buildings through CFD modelling and subsequently used for data-driven surrogate model training, validation and testing. For the 
surrogate modelling, an attention-based neural network model (TabNet) was used. The following conclusions were drawn from the 
results of this study. 
• Attention-based neural networks like TabNet are competent in modelling unsteady wind pressure on rectangular tall buildings. The 
model achieved an R2 score of 0.983 for training and validation, and a score of 0.980 for predictions on the test set (unseen tall 
buildings). The time taken for a new CFD simulation varied between 94 and 120 h whereas the TabNet model predicts unsteady 
wind pressure in 10–20 s.
• The TabNet-predicted time-dependent wind pressure closely followed the wind pressure data obtained from CFD modelling. 
Changes in geometric parameters like slenderness ratio and side ratio, and wind direction were accurately captured by the data- 
driven model compared to the CFD data. Moreover, sudden changes in the time-dependent pressure, including intermittent 
peak pressure values, were accurately captured by the data-driven model.
• The instantaneous pressure contours on tall buildings in the test set revealed that the model is capable of identifying and recon-
structing special flow features such as intermittent vortex formation, flow separation, diagonal bands of vortices under oblique 
winds, flow reattachment, and steep pressure gradients.
• In addition, statistical quantities such as mean, standard deviation, and extreme values of the TabNet-predicted time histories were 
compared with the corresponding values obtained from the wind tunnel data. In particular, the mean and fluctuating wind 
pressures showed a good match between the wind tunnel data and surrogate model predictions.
• For the peak pressure, the surrogate model reasonably captured the distribution (generalised extreme value) of extreme events in 
the wind pressure values compared to the corresponding distribution of extremes obtained from the wind tunnel data. However, the 
peak pressure coefficients predicted by the data-driven surrogate model tend to be slightly conservative near the edges and corners 
of the building.
• Overall, this study demonstrated good agreement between the wind tunnel results, CFD results, and TabNet (surrogate) model 
predictions, showing that the data-driven model achieves acceptable accuracy for wind engineering applications. It is noteworthy 
that the surrogate model does not replace wind tunnel experiments or numerical modelling, rather underpins engineering prac-
titioners by providing fast and reliable predictions of unsteady wind pressure on tall buildings.
