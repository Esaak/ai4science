Abstract
Urban greenery constitutes a primary strategy for mitigating the Urban Heat Island (UHI)
eﬀect, yet its inﬂuence on ventilation and pollutant dispersion remains a complex phe-
nomenon. While Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) provides the necessary ﬁdelity
to analyse these interactions, its high computational cost limits its use in large-scale ur-
ban design. Emerging data-driven surrogate models oﬀer an eﬃcient solution, but their
accuracy depends completely on the quality and representativeness of the training data.
To address this challenge, this thesis develops a comprehensive CFD framework designed
to serve as a data generator for machine-learning emulators. The canonical street-canyon
conﬁguration is extended to incorporate a robust physical model of vegetation by treating
it as a porous medium. This formulation couples aerodynamic drag and turbulence pro-
duction with energy exchanges associated with evapotranspirative cooling and dynamic
shading from the surrounding structures and vegetation.
A parametric study is conducted to quantify the trade-oﬀs between thermal regulation and
air quality. The simulations reveal that while conﬁgurations with dense foliage succeed in
lowering street-level temperatures, they also obstruct airﬂow, leading to a trapping eﬀect
that signiﬁcantly reduces ventilation. Furthermore, secondary buoyancy-driven vortices
generated by wall heating and vegetation fundamentally alter dispersion patterns. This
work presents a CFD model capable of producing the foundational dataset necessary to
extend the input space of existing AI surrogates to enable holistic urban microclimate
prediction.

1 Introduction
Air quality in urban areas is a growing concern due to its impact on public health and the
environment, with street canyons being especially vulnerable to pollutant accumulation.
The characteristic vortices generated within them obstruct eﬀective ventilation and trap
contaminants at street level. These ventilation ineﬃciencies are especially severe in the
worst-case ventilation conﬁguration, which consists of a square street canyon subjected
to wind perpendicular to the street axis, strengthening recirculation and signiﬁcantly
reducing air exchange. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is a key tool for analysing
these complex environments, but its high computational cost limits large-scale studies.
Data-driven models oﬀer an eﬃcient alternative by emulating the complex physics of
ﬂow, heat transfer and pollutant dispersion; however, existing models often omit the
critical impact of vegetation. The objective of this thesis is to bridge this gap by applying
established vegetation parameterisations within the CFD model, thereby generating the
necessary data to enhance these surrogate models for more realistic urban planning.
1.1 Motivation
Improving air quality in urban environments has become an urgent necessity, as the World
Health Organization (WHO) identiﬁes it as the greatest environmental threat to public
well-being. With global urban populations projected to reach 68% by 2050 [1], major
cities around the world are already facing critical levels of pollution, leading to signiﬁcant
impacts on public health. The continuous growth in urban density and traﬃc worsens
this issue, making the ability to assess, predict and mitigate pollution an essential tool for
sustainable urban planning.
To analyse these complex urban environments, CFD simulations are a widely established
scientiﬁc tool, but their elevated computational cost limits their practical application.
Data-driven models oﬀer an eﬃcient alternative to this challenge, emulating CFD results
at a signiﬁcantly lower cost. However, the predictive power of these models is entirely
dependent on the quality of their training data. A signiﬁcant gap in most of the current
approaches, including those recently developed, is the omission of urban vegetation. As
trees constantly alter ﬂow conditions, their absence in the training datasets creates a blind
spot in the resulting predictions. Addressing this gap is essential for the development of
truly holistic models.

1.2 State of the Art
To accurately model the inﬂuence of vegetation on urban environments, CFD simulations
typically incorporate speciﬁc parameterisations. Vegetation is commonly represented as
a porous medium that interacts with the surrounding airﬂow, requiring the inclusion of
additional terms in the governing equations of ﬂuid dynamics.
The primary aerodynamic eﬀect of vegetation is the reduction in wind speed caused by
drag from foliage. This is modelled by adding a sink term to the momentum equation.
The foundational formulations describing the eﬀects of vegetation on both mean ﬂow and
turbulence through these additional terms were ﬁrst presented in seminal works by Green
[2], Liu et al. [3] and Sanz [4].
Building on this framework, Buccolieri et al. [5] provided a key advancement for practical
applications in urban areas. Through a combination of wind tunnel experiments and
numerical simulations, they introduced a methodology to empirically determine the drag
eﬀects of the canopy. They characterised the vegetation’s resistance to airﬂow using a
pressure loss coeﬃcient, an inertial loss term based on direct measurements of porous
materials in a wind tunnel. This approach provided a robust method for parameterising
the momentum sink of trees in CFD simulations.
Beyond simply reducing wind speed, vegetation also alters the turbulence levels of the
ﬂow. The wakes generated by leaves and branches enhance turbulence, which must be
accounted for in turbulence modelling. This is handled by adding source terms to the
governing equations for the turbulent quantities.
As detailed by Amorim et al. [6], standard ﬂow equations were extended to incorporate the
aerodynamic eﬀects of trees on the wind ﬁeld. Their work focused on assessing how this
modiﬁed wind ﬂow aﬀects the dispersion of carbon monoxide from road traﬃc. By adding
these turbulence source terms, their model signiﬁcantly improved accuracy in predicting
pollutant concentrations at the pedestrian level compared to simulations that neglected
vegetation eﬀects.
More recently, Shen et al. [7] also applied this approach to investigate how diﬀerent
vegetation planting patterns aﬀect pollutant distribution within a street canyon. Their
numerical study conﬁrmed that these source terms are essential for capturing the complex
changes in airﬂow and pollutant dispersion caused by the presence of trees and greenbelts.

Vegetation also plays a crucial role in regulating the urban thermal environment, primarily
by providing shade that reduces solar heating of surfaces. Furthermore, the cooling eﬀect
of evapotranspiration is represented by introducing a sink term in the energy equation.
Authors like Gromke et al. [8] and Moradpour et al. [9] introduced a constant volumetric
cooling term to model the transpirational heat removal in vegetated regions. While this
simpliﬁed method is eﬀective in certain scenarios, other physically-based models are also
commonly used. For instance, the parameterisation developed by Bruse et al. [10] and
applied by authors such as Yang et al. [11] and Defraeye et al. [12] models the thermal
eﬀect as a heat ﬂux term dynamically linked to local conditions. It deﬁnes heat transfer
as a function of leaf area density ( LAD), local wind speed and the temperature diﬀerence
between foliage and ambient air, providing more accurate solutions of the plant-atmosphere
energy exchange.
This body of work is comprehensively reviewed by Buccolieri et al. [13], who summarise
the state-of-the-art parameterisations used in CFD to model the aerodynamic, deposition
and thermal eﬀects of urban vegetation. Their review conﬁrms that this framework is
the most eﬀective method for integrating the complex inﬂuence of vegetation into urban
environmental simulations.
1.3 Objectives
The principal objective of this thesis is to develop a high-ﬁdelity CFD model capable of
simulating the microclimate within an urban street canyon, with a speciﬁc focus on inte-
grating the eﬀects of vegetation. Three interconnected strands of work support this core
aim. First, the model will incorporate state-of-the-art parameterisations for vegetation,
treating it as a porous medium, alongside accurate energy boundary conditions to capture
solar loading and thermal exchange. Second, the predictive reliability of the developed
framework will be evaluated through validation against a benchmark case for vegetated
scenarios, which provides both wind tunnel measurements and reference CFD data for
comparison. Finally, the validated model will be employed in a targeted parametric study
to provide a preliminary quantiﬁcation of the inﬂuence of key variables on the resultant
ﬁelds. The outcome of this work is to provide the basis for generating a robust dataset,
designed to enhance the physical ﬁdelity of training data for machine learning-based sur-
rogate models of the urban environment.

2 Methodology
2.1 Physical Model
This thesis presents a comprehensive numerical investigation based on two-dimensional
stationary Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) simulations. The primary objective
is to quantify the impact of urban vegetation on the microclimatic conditions and pollutant
dispersion within an idealised street canyon.
This work extends the methodology developed by Moreira et al. [14] by integrating a
comprehensive vegetation parameterisation, including all physical processes described later
in Section 2.2.3. Within the scope of this thesis, the focus is placed on developing the
foundation of a database by modelling vegetation eﬀects and reﬁning the energy boundary
conditions through a heat-ﬂux-based approach, assessing their inﬂuence on the ﬂow and
pollutant dispersion. This approach provides a basis for more accurate simulations of
urban microclimates and for future studies in street canyon environments.
The study is conducted in the computational domain illustrated in Figure 2.1. The geome-
try represents a canonical urban environment and is designed to ensure a robust numerical
solution independent of boundary eﬀects.

A pollutant line source is deﬁned at street level, centred on the canyon ﬂoor and located
0.2 metres above ground level. It also acts as a heat source, representing the waste heat

street canyon as rectangular porous zones. Trees are deﬁned by their crown width ( Wt),

centreline from its adjacent wall, as illustrated in Figure 2.1.

2.3 Numerical Setup
The numerical model has been solved using the Finite Element Method (FEM) imple-
mented in COMSOL Multiphysics v6.3 [23], following the same setup described by Mor-
eira et al. [14]. The same discretisation order (P1+P1) and SUPG stabilisation with
streamline and crosswind diﬀusion have been applied.
Regarding the mesh, a mapped structured grid has been generated in the street-canyon
region due to the inclusion of vegetation elements. This area has been reﬁned using
square elements with a characteristic length of 0.1 m to better capture the local ﬂow
and scalar gradients. The rest of the computational domain has been meshed with a
coarser structured quadrilateral grid, ensuring smooth transitions between regions and
maintaining computational eﬃciency.
As noted in Section 2.1, the computational domain has been deﬁned using the same
methodology as in Moreira et al. [14], which complies with the recommended guidelines for
CFD modelling of urban ﬂows [24]. The ﬁnal grid contains 84,032 quadrilateral elements,
providing adequate resolution in the canyon region.

3 Results
3.1 Model Validation
Before directly addressing the research questions of this thesis, model validation is nec-
essary to ensure the reliability of the numerical setup. The objective of this validation
is to demonstrate that the model can accurately reproduce physical phenomena under
controlled conditions. This is achieved by comparing the simulation results with estab-
lished experimental data, verifying that the model can faithfully replicate complex airﬂow
patterns around vegetation.
For this purpose, the validation procedure is based on the methodology presented by Liang
et al. [25]. This involves numerically replicating their wind tunnel study of airﬂow through
a small forest stand. A key feature of this study is that, rather than treating LAD and
the drag coeﬃcient as constants, the model deﬁnes both as functions of height, allowing
for a more realistic diﬀerentiation between the aerodynamic behaviour of the tree trunks
and that of the upper canopy. Successfully reproducing the results of this experiment,
the chosen numerical schemes, turbulence models and vegetation parameterisations are
veriﬁed as appropriate for this ﬁeld of study.

vegetation centrally positioned along this depth and occupying a 5-metre-wide strip. This
distinction is important to note, as the dimensionality of the vegetation may inﬂuence the
ﬂow and should be considered when interpreting the results.

3.2 Convergence
3.2.1 Mesh Convergence
A mesh convergence analysis has been conducted monitoring the average concentration at
the leeward wall. Due to the complex source terms introduced to model vegetation and
heat transfer, coarse meshes failed to resolve local gradients, leading to solver divergence;
consequently, only reﬁned grids capable of sustaining physical solutions are presented. As
shown in Figure 3.3a, the solution stabilises after overcoming initial sensitivity. A grid of
84,032 elements has been selected, yielding a relative error of less than 0.4% compared to
the ﬁnest reference. This conﬁguration has been chosen to guarantee the stability of the
model while maintaining computational eﬃciency.
3.2.2 Domain Convergence
To ensure the independence of the results from the computational boundaries, a speciﬁc
convergence analysis has been performed. Since the upstream and downstream lengths
have been established as suﬃcient to resolve the ﬂow development and the wake regions,
the study has focused on the domain height. The objective has been to determine a
vertical clearance large enough to prevent artiﬁcial acceleration of the ﬂow due to the top
boundary. As illustrated in Figure 3.3b, diﬀerent domain heights have been evaluated,
studying the variation in ﬂow variables. A ﬁnal domain height of 8 H has been selected,

the blockage eﬀect is negligible.

4 Conclusions
This thesis has developed a comprehensive CFD framework to simulate the complex mi-
croclimatic interactions within an urban street canyon. A robust physical model treating
vegetation as a porous medium has been successfully implemented, integrating momen-
tum sinks for aerodynamic drag, turbulence sources for canopy-induced mixing and energy
terms for evapotranspirative cooling, as well as an accurate shading model. The numerical
setup has been validated against wind tunnel experimental data and other RANS models,
demonstrating strong agreement for mean velocity and TKE proﬁles.
The parametric study has revealed a critical trade-oﬀ in the ﬂow dynamics of the street
canyon. While high LAD conﬁgurations have been shown to signiﬁcantly mitigate heat
stress, they have simultaneously been observed to obstruct airﬂow, leading to a trapping
eﬀect where pollutant concentrations increase, particularly near the leeward wall. Further-
more, in speciﬁc thermal scenarios, wall heating has been identiﬁed to trigger a secondary
buoyant vortex. Although not included in the presented results, additional simulations
indicate that under certain parameter combinations this thermally induced recirculation
can strengthen considerably, potentially raising pollutant concentrations at pedestrian
level and on the windward side.
Ultimately, this work establishes a foundational framework for generating physically en-
riched datasets required to advance data-driven modelling in urban environments. By
extending the parametric space to include vegetation, this framework enables the future
training of machine-learning surrogate models.
4.1 Future Work
Future research should concentrate on developing a high-ﬁdelity CFD database that spans
the extended parameter space, providing the foundation for training surrogate models
aimed at accelerating urban microclimate predictions. Another promising direction in-
volves using these surrogates to systematically identify critical ﬂow regimes, particularly
those produced by speciﬁc combinations of wall heating and vegetation that give rise to
secondary buoyant vortices or canopy-induced ﬂow separation. In addition, a global sen-
sitivity analysis should be undertaken to quantify the relative inﬂuence of thermal and
vegetative parameters, thereby informing dimensionality reduction and the construction
of more eﬃcient predictive frameworks.
