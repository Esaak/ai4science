ABSTRACT
Fine particulate matter (PM 2.5) is a common air pollutant that represents a signi ﬁcant threat to public health. In
Dublin, a major source of PM 2.5 is diesel exhaust, brake dust, and rubber tire particles from vehicles. Particulate
matter can be deposited onto the surface of vegetation thereby removing it from the atmosphere. The presence of
trees can increase the amount of surface area on which deposition can occur, but they can also alter wind ﬂow in
a way that increases local pollutant concentrations. This study assesses the eﬀ ect of urban trees on removing
PM2.5 using a novel combination of air quality monitoring and the i-Tree Eco (UFORE) deposition model during
rush and non-rush hours with di ﬀerent traﬃc volumes. Monitoring was conducted in a tree alley and an area of
no vegetation along the Drumcondra Road Lower in Dublin. Results indicated that there was no di ﬀerence in
PM2.5 concentrations outside of rush hours, but concentrations were signi ﬁcantly lower in the tree alley during
rush hours compared to the street section with no trees. The i-Tree Eco model calculated that the tree alley could
remove approximately 3 kg of PM 2.5 on a yearly basis. Air monitoring found that the presence of trees resulted in
a maximum air quality improvement of 126 %, more than double what was predicted by i-Tree Eco. While this
study provides an initial exploration of trees and air quality in Dublin, further research combining these tech-
niques with the measurement of local wind speed and direction would allow for a better understanding of how
Dublin’s trees can a ﬀect its air quality. The results from this study suggest that they do cause a degree of air
quality improvement and can be used to inform national clean air strategies aimed at reducing pollutant
emissions.
1. Introduction
Air pollution is a growing environmental threat, with rising global
emissions threatening ecosystems and human health. Poor air quality
has become a major cause of premature death and illness and is the
largest environmental health risk ( Gakidou et al., 2017 ; HEI, 2018; Lim
et al., 2012 ; WHO, 2016 , 2014). Poor air quality is responsible for
approximately 1 in 9 deaths worldwide ( WHO, 2016 ). In 2015 alone,
422,000 people across Europe died from illnesses caused by exposure to
outdoor air pollution ( HEI, 2018 ).
Particulate matter is classi ﬁed as an air quality indicator based on
its eﬀects on public health and the environment ( EEA, 2017). PM refers
to solid and liquid aerosolized particles suspended in the atmosphere.
Sources of PM can be natural, such as volcanic eruptions and sand-
storms, or anthropogenic, such as fuel combustion and processing in-
dustries ( Farmer, 2002 ). PM is the air pollutant most hazardous to
human health because when inhaled, it can accumulate onto lung tissue
and even enter the bloodstream, creating a major risk for
cardiovascular and pulmonary heart disease ( Cen, 2015 ), stroke,
chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer ( WHO, 2013 ), re-
spiratory infections in children ( Brauer et al., 2002), and worsen ex-
isting respiratory conditions such as asthma ( 2002, Brauer et al., 2007 )
and cystic ﬁbrosis ( Goeminne et al., 2013 ). These particulates are ca-
tegorized according to their size. PM 10 is particulate matter that has a
particle diameter up to 10 μm, and PM 2.5 particles have a diameter of
up to 2.5 μm. Smaller particles are more health hazardous as they can
penetrate into deeper parts of the lung ( CDC, 2019 ).
The WHO has published guidelines on limits to PM 2.5 concentra-
tions and has stated that concentrations above a yearly mean of 10 μg/
m3 and a 24 h mean of 25 μg/m3 represents an increased health risk to
exposed populations ( WHO, 2006 ). The European Union has im-
plemented an air quality directive (2008/50/EC Directive on Ambient
Air Quality and Cleaner Air for Europe) that sets a limit of 40 μg/m3 for
yearly average PM 2.5 levels. However, concentrations below these
thresholds are not classi ﬁed as ‘safe’, and there is little evidence to
suggest that there is any limit below which there are no associated

In Ireland, 1180 premature deaths in 2018 were caused due to poor
air quality ( EPA, 2019 ). The country ’s main anthropogenic sources of
PM2.5 are road traﬃ c and the burning of solid fuel for home heating
(EPA, 2019). Road tra ﬃc in particular accounts for up to 73 % of
particulate matter levels ( GLA, 2006). Particles caused by vehicle traﬃ c
include resuspended particles of tire rubber, brake dust, and diesel
exhaust particles that result from incomplete combustion ( Yanosky,
Tonne, Beevers, Wilkinson, & Kelly, 2012; Basu, Alam, Ghosh, Gill, &
McNabola, 2019). Traﬃc-related PM is more toxic to humans compared
to PM from other sources, potentially due to the metal content in brakes
(Yanosky et al., 2012 ). PM concentrations in Dublin have been observed
to be almost twice those observed in Cork city which is the second
largest city in Ireland, most likely due to higher amounts of re-
suspended road dust resulting from larger tra ﬃc volumes ( Jennings
et al., 2006).
Air pollution is closely tied to urbanisation. Higher population
density, industrial activity, and large tra ﬃc volumes all contribute to
poor air quality in urban centres ( Fenger, 1999). Currently, over 3.5
billion people live in cities, with this ﬁgure expected to reach 6.5 billion
by 2050 (UN, 2019). This rapid urban growth will require greater use of
mitigation strategies to reduce the associated impact on air quality. One
such strategy studied by researchers has been the use of urban vege-
tation for air quality improvement ( Abhijith & Gokhale, 2015 ; Abhijith
& Kumar, 2019 ; Beckett, Freer‐Smith, & Taylor, 2000 ; Nowak, Crane, &
Stevens, 2006 ).
Urban trees inﬂuence local air quality by altering the deposition and
dispersion rates of pollutants. Dispersion is the process by which air
pollutants are transported and diluted by wind currents. The presence
of buildings and vegetation can disrupt local air ﬂows and alter dis-
persion patterns in that area. These patterns are usually studied using
wind tunnels and computational ﬂuid dynamic models. A common
feature of cities is the street canyon which is comprised of rows of
buildings along both sides of a road. Street canyons can di ﬀer in size
and aspect ratio, measured as the ratio of the canyon ’s height to width.
When an air current passes over a street canyon at a parallel or oblique
angle, it forms a vortex within the canyon with a ground wind ﬂowing
in the opposite direction as the rooftop current ( Oke, 1987). This vortex
can result in PM concentrations being up to 12 –146 % higher on the
leeward side of the street canyon depending on the wind direction and
aspect ratio ( Abhijith & Gokhale, 2015 ; Gromke & Ruck, 2012; Vos,
Maiheu, Vankerkom, & Janssen, 2013 ; Wania, Bruse, Blond, & Weber,
2012).
Trees present inside the canyon can alter the air ﬂow, increasing
or decreasing pollutant concentrations in certain areas depending on
wind direction, the size and arrangement of the trees, and the size and
shape of the canyon ( Abhijith et al., 2017 ; Gromke & Ruck, 2012;
Gromke, Buccolieri, Di Sabatino, & Ruck, 2008 ; Oke, 1987 ).
Trees can act as a barrier between the pollutant source and the re-
ceptors. Based on the standard k- ε based computational ﬂuid dynamics
model, Hong, Lin, and Qin (2017) found that the pollution concentra-
tion can be reduced up to 85 % due to trees located close to the pol-
lution source. However, Janhäll (2015) documented that even though
vegetation such as hedges can ﬁlter out PM when located close to a
pollutant source, trees and other higher canopy vegetation can increase
the pollution concentration inside a street canyon by reducing turbu-
lence and mixing of fresh air with polluted air inside the canyon.
Fieldwork experiments at six street canyons conducted by Jin, Guo,
Wheeler, Kan, and Che (2014) at central Shanghai showed an increase
of up to 30 % of PM 2.5 concentration near the leeward walls of the
buildings due to trees. Both wind tunnel experiments ( Gromke & Ruck,
2007) as well as numerical simulation models ( Buccolieri, Gromke, Di
Sabatino, & Ruck, 2009 ; Gromke et al., 2008) obtained similar results.
In general, the majority of the pollution dispersion models only
account for the aerodynamic e ﬀect and omit the deposition e ﬀect
(Janhäll, 2015). Field studies ( Di Sabatino, Buccolieri, Pappaccogli, &
Leo, 2015; Ortolani & Vitale, 2016; Petroﬀ, Mailliat, Amielh, &
Anselmet, 2008; Vong, Vong, Vickers, & Covert, 2010 ; Yang, Yu, &
Gong, 2008 ) and wind tunnel experiment ( Huang, Lin, Khlystov, &
Katul, 2013 ; Lin & Khlystov, 2012 ; Räsänen et al., 2013 ) noted that
deposition e ﬀect on vegetation can have a signi ﬁcant e ﬀect in the re-
moval of PM concentration. Xue and Li (2017) evaluated aerodynamic
and deposition e ﬀects of roadside trees based on an isolated street
canyon case. They noted that higher leaf area density (LAD) and a high
deposition rate can remove 39– 89 % of air pollution. Buccolieri,
Jeanjean, Gatto, and Leigh (2018) considered the OpenFOAM Compu-
tational Fluid Dynamics model and simulate several scenarios to eval-
uate the relative importance of both aerodynamic and deposition e ﬀects
of trees. Based on the results they concluded that deposition e ﬀect may
become crucial by dominating over the aerodynamic e ﬀects that lead to
a decrease in pollution concentration. Ottosen and Kumar (2020) con-
duct a ﬁeld study in front and behind a hedge on the edge of a street and
noted that deposition can lead to a reduction of pollutant concentration
up to 52 %. They further noted that wind direction has a minor con-
tribution to pollution reduction. Tiwari et al. (2019) conducted a cri-
tical review of di ﬀerent modelling approaches to assess the impacts of
green infrastructure on air pollutants dispersion at micro- and macro-
scales.
Airborne particles can be deposited onto vegetation and manmade
materials as they pass close by their surface. The rate of deposition is
also known as the deposition velocity. When particles are deposited
onto leaves and branches, they are retained temporarily and are either
washed o ﬀ by rain ( Przybysz, Sæbø, Hanslin, & Gawro ński, 2014),
transported to the soil as plant parts fall to the ground, or blown o ﬀ by
wind and resuspended in the atmosphere ( Nowak, Hirabayashi, Bodine,
& Greenﬁeld, 2014). Vegetation tends to have a greater surface area per
unit volume compared to manmade materials, with signi ﬁcantly higher
deposition rates observed on grass compared to glass and cement sur-
faces ( Roupsard et al., 2013 ). Trees are considered the most e ﬀective
type of air- ﬁltering vegetation due to their large total leaf area
(McDonald et al., 2007). The species of plant a ﬀects deposition velocity
and air ﬁltration due to di ﬀering spatial arrangements of branches and
leaves. Leaves with features such as hairs, grooves, and veins have a
higher surface and are also associated with increased deposition ( Sæbø
et al., 2012 ; Weber, Kowarik, & Säumel, 2014 ).

Particle deposition on vegetation surfaces is inﬂ uenced by several
elements, including the diameter and shape of the particles, the spatial
structure of the trees, and meteorological conditions. Therefore, factors
such as humidity, wind speed, plant species, and planting con ﬁguration
aﬀect the deposition velocity and ﬁltration e ﬀectiveness of plants
(Litschke & Kuttler, 2008). One of the ways in which the pollution re-
duction capability of vegetation is assessed is by using dry deposition
models ( Yang, Chang, & Yan, 2015 ). Dry deposition is the process by
which atmospheric particles are deposited onto surfaces without in-
volving precipitation ( Lovett & Kinsman, 1990 ; Ruijrok, Davidson, &
Nicholson, 1995), and depends on the particle size, pollutant con-
centration, and the reactivity between the particulates and the im-
pacted surface ( Lindberg, Harriss, & Turner, 1982). The Urban Forest
Eﬀects (UFORE) model was developed by Nowak and Crane (2000) to
estimate the quantity of air pollutants captured by vegetation through
dry deposition. More recently, a software application called i-Tree Eco
was introduced as a user friendly, easily accessible version of the
UFORE model ( Hirabayashi, Kroll, & Nowak, 2012 ).
i-Tree Eco is an open-access software tool developed by the United
States Forest Service (USFS). It analyzes the urban forest structure in
terms of species composition, leaf area, and tree health, and uses these
parameters to estimate the ecosystem services provided by those trees.
i-Tree Eco can quantify the amount of air pollution that is removed by a
particular stand of trees or entire urban forest on a yearly basis for
common air pollutants including PM 2.5 (Nowak & Crane, 2000).
i-Tree Eco uses a dry deposition component built into the UFORE
model to calculate the pollution removal by plants during non-pre-
cipitation periods and the associated improvement in air quality on an
hourly basis for a one-year period ( Hirabayashi, Kroll, & Nowak, 2015).
It can also distinguish between the air pollution reduction provided by
diﬀerent tree species and includes a list of species that the model can
analyze. The software requires local meteorological data and pollutant
concentration levels as well as tree size and species data to perform its
calculations. i-Tree Eco has an inbuilt international database that

Many studies have demonstrated that urban trees help reduce air
pollutant concentrations, with researchers having used i-Tree to esti-
mate the ecosystem services provided by urban forests of major cities
worldwide. Compiling data for an entire city ’s trees requires dividing
the forest into plots and sampling each tree within randomly selected
plots ( i-Tree Eco Users Manual , 2019 ). Baró et al. (2014) used this
procedure with i-Tree Eco to estimate that Barcelona ’s urban forest
removes 305,000 kg of air pollutants each year. Saunders, Dade, and
Van Niel (2011) ran the UFORE model in Australia to quantify the
amount of carbon sequestration, air pollutant removal, and biogenic
volatile organic compound emissions associated with Perth ’s urban
forest. Selmi et al. (2016) used i-Tree Eco to estimate the amount of air
pollution removed by urban trees in Strasbourg city, comparing the
results with local emissions levels and determining that the city ’s public
trees removed 7% of the emitted PM 10 in Strasbourg. This study was the
ﬁrst to use i-Tree Eco in France. Kiss, Takács, Pogácsás, and Gulyás
(2015) ran the i-Tree Eco model on the trees in the centre of Szeged,
Hungary. They identi ﬁed two similarly shaped parallel streets with
Street A having a stand of old-growth trees and Street B having been
clear cut of the same species and planted with younger trees. After
running the model, they found that Street A ’s trees were each removing
256.8 g of pollutants per year, compared with the 8.5 g of pollutants
that each tree in Street B was removing. Jayasooriya, Ng,
Muthukumaran, and Perera (2017) compared the air pollutant removal
capacities of trees, green roofs, and green walls in Melbourne using i-
Tree Eco, determining that trees removed the most pollutants out of the
three types of green infrastructure.
Most studies investigating the relationship between air quality and
urban trees have relied on models rather than experimental data ( Yin
et al., 2011). However, some research has also been conducted using air
quality sensors and analyzing experimental data. In their study quan-
tifying air pollutant removal by urban parks in Shanghai, China, Yin
et al. (2011) monitored air quality outside and inside six public parks in
Shanghai and concluded that parks could remove traﬃ c pollutants at
ground level by up to 35 % for PM. Bodnaruk et al. (2017) considered
spatially explicit biophysical models to explore strategies for tree
planting and noted that an increase in tree cover from current (24 %) to
potential maximum (44.4 %) in Baltimore, USA can lead to pollution
removal from 133− 139 t/year. Cavanagh, Zawar-Reza, and Wilson
(2009) used air quality sensors to investigate PM 10 levels inside an
urban park in Christchurch, New Zealand. The primary source of PM 10
pollutants in the area was from domestic wood burners, and they found
that concentrations inside the park were an average of 50 % lower than
those outside of it. Shan et al. (2007) measured PM concentrations in a
street in Pudong, China, that had 15 m wide greenbelts on both sides.
They determined that the trees along the road were removing up to 70
% of suspended PM, and that these greenbelts should be at least 5 m
wide for optimal performance. Vailshery, Jaganmohan, and Nagendra
(2013) identiﬁed ten high-tra ﬃc roads in Bangalore, India, that had
adjacent segments such that one segment had at least 150 m of tree
cover and the other segment had no trees, and monitored the air quality
in each segment. They found that although traﬃ c levels were equal
among all sampling sites, the segments without trees had twice the
average PM concentration than those with trees.
The aims of this study are twofold. The ﬁrst is to determine if and
how PM2.5 concentrations diﬀer between two di ﬀerent urban settings: a
tree-lined street canyon and one without trees at di ﬀerent times of the
day with varying traﬃ c ﬂow. The goal is to investigate the e ﬀectiveness
of trees in the removal of air pollution during rush hour and non-rush
hour periods. It can be considered that during rush hours the major
source of air pollution is the high tra ﬃc volume, while in the non-rush
hour period air pollution mostly re ﬂect the background concentration
of the locality. The second is to use i-Tree Eco with the street canyon ’s
trees and attempt to validate the model using results obtained from air
quality monitoring, which, to the knowledge of the authors, are not yet
explored by many researchers earlier.
The following objectives were formulated in achieving the above
research aims:
1 Monitoring PM 2.5 concentrations in a street canyon setting with
trees and one without trees at both rush hours and non-rush hours
time period.
2 Identifying trends and statistically signi ﬁcant di ﬀerences between
air quality results from the two locations by investigating the e ﬀect
of trees in reducing air pollution.
3 Creating an inventory of the trees in the selected tree alley and using
i-Tree Eco to calculate the pollutant removal rates associated with
that stand of trees.
4 Analyze both sets of results to determine how the i-Tree Eco model
predictions compare with the actual PM 2.5 concentrations obtained
during air quality monitoring.
The main goal of this study is to investigate whether the presence of
trees has the potential to remove traﬃ c-fed and background air pollu-
tion through deposition and improve the air quality in an urban street
canyon.
2. Methodology
2.1. Site description
The monitoring area was a 300 m long section of Drumcondra Road
Lower in the northern part of Dublin, Ireland ( Fig. 1). The southernmost
150 m section of this road is devoid of any vegetation and is lined by
two and three-storey commercial buildings on each side of the road.
There are three lanes of regular traﬃ c, a bus lane, and a right-turn lane.
This area comprises the ‘non-tree’ part of the study area. The upper 150
m of the road is a tree alley made up of large, old-growth Norway maple
(Acer platanoides) trees, which were planted between the road and
footpath. These trees are widespread in Dublin with 14 –23 % of the
city’s public trees belonging to the maple species ( Ningal, Mills, &
Smithwick, 2010 ; Pauleit et al., 2002), so this location was re-
presentative of many other tree alleys in the city. Most of the tree alley
is made up of trees planted symmetrically on both sides of the road
except for the southern end, which has ﬁve trees on the western side of
the road only. The road is lined by three-story residential buildings,
most of which had no shrubs or trees in the private yards directly in
front of them. This part of the road is made up of 3 lanes of regular
traﬃc and a bus lane.
This road was chosen as the monitoring site as it complied with the
following criteria:
(a) Part of the street had few to no trees planted along it and part of the
street was a ‘tree alley ’, having large trees planted at regular in-
tervals along each side of the road. A tree alley made up of old,
large trees would be representative of the maximum density of trees
and canopy cover in an urban street setting in Dublin. This street
layout would allow for the comparison between a road section with
trees and one without trees.
(b) The street was laid out along the same direction, i.e. it was not
winding or curved. This would reduce the potential di ﬀerence in air
pollutant concentrations due to wind speed and direction, since
both the ‘tree’ and ‘non-tree’ parts of the road would be aligned in
the same direction and presumably receive similar wind conditions.
(c) Most of the trees in the ‘tree’ section of the road were on public
property. This would allow access to measure their Diameter at
breast height (DBH) which would then be used to run the i-Tree Eco
model, ensuring that most local trees would be captured by the
model.

(d) The street received fairly heavy traﬃ c, with similar traﬃ c levels
along the ‘tree’ and ‘non-tree’ parts of the road. Combustion from
vehicle engines contributes to higher PM 2.5 concentrations in areas
of heavy traﬃ c( Squadrito, Cueto, Dellinger, & Pryor, 2001 ). Since
deposition occurs at a higher rate when there are higher levels of air
pollutants (Nowak & Crane, 2000 ), trees would be expected to have
a more signiﬁcant eﬀect on air quality in areas where the air quality
is poor.
(e) Both the ‘tree’ and ‘non-tree’ parts of the street had similar aspect
ratios. The spatial geometry of a street can in ﬂuence wind patterns
and microclimates ( Buccolieri et al., 2011), which in turn can a ﬀect
pollutant concentrations. Choosing monitoring sites located in areas
with similar aspect ratios would reduce the impact of varying local
microclimates on pollutant levels.
2.2. Data collection
Air quality monitoring was conducted in two locations along
Drumcondra Road Lower ( Fig. 1). The monitors were placed outdoors
in the approximate midpoint of the ‘tree’ and ‘non-tree’ sections of the
road so they would be representative of the air quality in the parts of
the road with and without vegetation. Both sensors were placed on the
same side of the road approximately 4 m from the edge of the street so
that they would be a ﬀected similarly if a primary vortex were to form in
the street canyon.
Air quality monitoring was conducted using two Smart Citizen 2.1
sensors placed along Drumcondra Road Lower. The Smart Citizen sen-
sors are low-cost air quality sensors that were created to raise aware-
ness on environmental issues and engage citizens in monitoring their
environment. The current version 2.1 uses a Plantower PMS 5003
sensor to measure PM 2.5 levels in the atmosphere. This sensor shines a
light beam towards a light detector and the amount of deﬂ ected light is
measured by a photometer and used to derive the mass of airborne
particles ( Camprodon et al., 2019 ; Kumar, Omidvarborna, Pilla, &
Lewin, 2020). Particles with a diameter of less than 0.3 μm are too
small to scatter enough light and are therefore not detected by this
sensor technology ( Koehler & Peters, 2015; Thomas & Gebhart, 1994 ).
The Plantower PMS sensors have been evaluated for accuracy by
Badura, Batog, Drzeniecka-Osiadacz, and Modzel (2018) and their
readings were found to be highly reproducible between units. Sayahi,
Butterﬁeld, and Kelly (2019) tested the performance of PMS sensors in
outdoor conditions and found that the sensor readings correlated well
with reference monitors in the winter but less so at other times of the
year. The PMS 5003 sensors were included in the Smart Citizen mon-
itoring kit as sensor benchmarking carried out by other investigators
indicated that these were an acceptable low-cost option ( Camprodon
et al., 2019 ; Jayaratne, Liu, Thai, Dunbabin, & Morawska, 2018 ; Rai
et al., 2017).
Monitoring was conducted to record air pollutant concentrations
(including PM 2.5), meteorological data, and noise levels every minute.
The Smart Citizen sensors were developed as part of the iSCAPE project,
a Horizon 2020-funded project aimed at controlling air quality and
carbon emissions in European cities ( Firus, Prampolini, & Simeone,
2017).
Air quality monitoring was carried out between October 21, 2019
and October 30, 2019. Both monitors were run concurrently for periods
of 45− 75 min at peak and low tra ﬃc times. Peak traﬃ c times were
identiﬁed as being between approximately 16:30 −18:30 from Monday
to Friday, with non-peak hours outside of these times. Observations of
the site during monitoring events con ﬁrmed that peak traﬃ c occurred
during these time periods. Preliminary monitoring on October 19, 2019
identiﬁed low air pollutant concentrations (1 –4 u g/m3) during non-
peak times, so most of the monitoring was conducted during peak
traﬃc hours. Results were obtained from six separate monitoring events
at peak traﬃ c times and three monitoring events at non-peak traﬃ c
times. A total of 1371 data points was collected during the monitoring
process.
The circumference of each tree in the tree alley was measured and
converted to DBH for later use by the i-Tree Eco model (Table S1 in
supplementary material).
2.3. i-Tree model setup
The guidelines provided by the i-Tree Eco Users Manual (2019)
were followed when collecting ﬁeld data of the study area. When set-
ting up the model, i-Tree Eco requires that the user specify whether
they are randomly sampling trees within a forest plot or if they are
working from a complete inventory of trees. Since the study area for
this research was relatively small, it was possible to create a complete
inventory of the trees in the area. The mandatory parameters that i-Tree
requires in order to calculate the air quality bene ﬁts are tree species and
DBH, which was collected through ﬁeldwork.
Local hourly weather and air quality data which was available in the
i-Tree Database tool was then fed into the model. Based on the model ’s
metadata, the PM2.5 concentration data used by the model was recorded
by the Dublin City Council ’s PM monitors located in Finglas, Dublin.
The meteorological data used by the model includes measurements of
wind speed, cloud ceiling height, temperature, air pressure, sky cover,
and precipitation taken from the Dublin Airport weather station located
approximately 6.5 km from the study site. The i-Tree Database had
meteorological and air quality data available for 2013, 2014, and 2015.
Once all the necessary data was input into the i-Tree Eco, the model was
run and provided estimates of the amount of PM 2.5 removed by the
street trees on Drumcondra Road Lower based on the air quality and
meteorological conditions in 2015, the most recent year. Additional
model runs provided estimates on PM 2.5 removal based on the outdoor
conditions for 2013 and 2014.
2.4. Data analysis
Analyses of di ﬀerences in PM 2.5 concentrations were conducted to
determine the statistical signi ﬁcance of the air quality results from the
tree alley and the ‘non-tree’ road segment. Statistical tests were con-
ducted using the Microsoft Excel Analysis Toolpak and R software.

3. Results
3.1. Exceedances of air quality guidelines
Results were obtained from a total of 744 min (sample points) of
monitoring in the non-tree road section and 627 min of monitoring at a
minute scale in the tree alley across a period of ﬁve days. Most of this
monitoring was done concurrently and the monitoring length dis-
crepancy was due to time delays in setting up and securing both
monitors, as well as instances where a monitor failed to record the
current PM 2.5 concentration.
Table 1 displays the number of individual sample points where the
air quality sensors recorded PM 2.5 concentrations exceeding 10, 20, and
25 μg/m3. Table 2 shows the number of sample points during rush
hours where the sensors recorded the same exceedances. On average,
PM2.5 concentrations were almost three times as likely to exceed 10 μg/
m3 in the non-tree road section than in the tree alley at any given
moment during monitoring. Exceedances of the 25 μg/m3 threshold
were over ﬁve times more likely to be recorded in the non-tree road
section.
3.2. Controlling for tra ﬃcd i ﬀerences
Two main assumptions were made when analyzing the data:
a) Climatic conditions were similar enough at monitoring locations so
that any minor di ﬀerences would have a negligible e ﬀect on PM 2.5
concentrations.
b) Noise levels were an indicator of traﬃ c volume. Since there was no
available recent map of Dublin tra ﬃc volume data, this assumption
was made on the basis that noise level data from the EPA (2018)
corresponded closely with Dublin ’s main roads. The highest noise
levels were recorded along motorways, suggesting that noise would
be a reliable indicator of traﬃ c volume.
The second assumption was required to control for any di ﬀerences
in traﬃ c that could a ﬀect local PM 2.5 concentrations. The ‘non-tree’
road section contains a right-turn lane that turns onto Clonli ﬀe Road, a
two-lane street perpendicular to Drumcondra Road Lower. Because the
two monitoring points were located on either side of this intersection,
there would have been some traﬃ c that passed through the lower
portion of Drumcondra Road Lower and turned onto Clonli ﬀe Road
without passing by the monitoring point in the tree alley. To account
for this traﬃ c discrepancy, the noise levels recorded by each monitor
were used as a proxy for the tra ﬃc volume passing by them. For each
pair of sample points (at the non-tree and tree alley locations), the
percent di ﬀerence in noise levels was obtained. The PM 2.5 concentra-
tion level in the ‘non-tree’ road segment was then reduced by this
percentage to control for the di ﬀerence in traﬃ c volume. Any resulting
diﬀerences in PM 2.5 concentrations between the non-tree and tree alley
locations could, therefore, be interpreted as not being a result of dif-
ferent traﬃ c levels.

3.4. i-Tree Eco model
There were 80 trees growing in the tree alley along Drumcondra
Road Lower. All were Norway maple trees. Tree DBH ranged between
47.4 cm and 100.2 cm, with an average diameter of approximately 70
cm (Table S1 in supplementary material). After inputting the ﬁeld tree
data from Table S1, the model was run three times using the meteor-
ological and air quality data for 2013, 2014, and 2015. The i-Tree Eco
model was able to estimate that, on average, this stand of trees removes
approximately 3 kg of PM 2.5 on a yearly basis. Fig. 7. shows the average
monthly amounts of PM 2.5 being removed due to the trees in the study
area.
Depending on weather and air quality conditions, the trees growing
along Drumcondra Road Lower could act to increase or decrease the
PM2.5 concentration. Using the meteorological and air quality data from
2015, i-Tree Eco estimated that, on an hourly basis, the tree alley was
responsible for a percent increase in air quality between −33.36 % and
51.34 % ( Fig. 8).
Table 3 shows the average hourly PM 2.5 concentrations recorded in
the non-tree road section and the tree alley during air quality mon-
itoring, as well as the unit di ﬀerence and percent di ﬀerence in con-

casionally worse in the tree alley, but these di ﬀerences were very minor
(less than 1 μg/m3 diﬀerence) and not observed after 13:00. During
rush hours, there was an average di ﬀerence between monitoring loca-

18:00, respectively. This translates to an increase in the air quality of
76.45 % and 128.69 % in the tree alley compared to the non-tree road
section.
4. Discussion
The air- ﬁltering e ﬀect of trees was evident when considering the
number of times that pollutant concentrations exceeded certain air
quality thresholds. PM 2.5 concentrations were above 10 μg/m3 for ap-
proximately one-third of the total time spent monitoring in the non-tree
road section ( Table 1). This threshold of 10 μg/m3 represents the
WHO’s yearly mean limit for PM 2.5. The same limit was exceeded by 14
% (88 times out of 627 data points) of the total monitoring time in the
tree alley ( Table 1). During rush hours ( Table 2), PM 2.5 concentrations
were above 10 μg/m3 for 58 % of the time in the non-tree road section
and 22 % of the time in the tree alley. The EU ’s yearly mean limit of 20
μg/m3 was exceeded for 15 % and 1.81 % of the time during rush hours
in the non-tree road section and the tree alley, respectively. Air quality
in Dublin is relatively high compared with that of other European cities

The PM 2.5 concentrations observed outside of rush hours were not
signiﬁcantly di ﬀerent between the non-tree road section and the tree
alley, and both hovered around 3 μg/m3. The fact that the concentra-
tions were relatively constant and similar suggests that it might re-

ambient level of air pollutants that are not a ﬀected by local sources of
air pollution ( Menichini, Iacovella, Monfredini, & Turrio-Baldassarri,
2007; WHO, 1980 ). Identifying the background concentration can
allow the di ﬀerentiation of new and local sources of pollution ( Gómez-
Losada, Pires, & Pino-Mejías, 2018 ). Any increases in PM 2.5 levels above
the background concentration can, therefore, be attributed to tra ﬃc
passing through the study area.
During rush hours, the average PM 2.5 level increased signi ﬁcantly in
both monitoring locations, though the mean concentration in the tree
alley was almost half of that of the concentration in the non-tree road
section. These results are consistent with the ﬁndings of Vailshery et al.
(2013), who determined that average PM 2.5 concentrations were twice
as high in road segments without trees when compared to road seg-
ments with trees in Bangalore, India. Meteorological data indicates that
during the ﬁve days of monitoring, average daily wind speeds varied
from 3.0 to 8.75 m/s and blew in from the west, east, and south ( Met
Éireann, 2019 ). Wind speed and direction in the monitoring locations
were not recorded as part of this study, though these factors are known
to in ﬂuence air pollutant concentrations ( Amorim, Rodrigues, Tavares,
Valente, & Borrego, 2013; Buccolieri et al., 2011; Gromke & Ruck,
2012; Jin et al., 2014 ; Vos et al., 2013 ). The e ﬀect of wind currents on
pollutant concentrations at the study site could not be isolated from the
eﬀect of the trees, however, the fact that PM 2.5 concentrations were
consistently lower in the tree alley during rush hours indicates that the
street trees had an air ﬁltering e ﬀect in the area when concentrations
were elevated, regardless of prevailing wind direction or speed. There
was no signi ﬁcant di ﬀerence between monitoring locations outside of
rush hours, however, since particle deposition is a process that is in-
ﬂuenced by the pollutant concentration, daytime PM 2.5 concentrations
may have been too low for there to be an observable di ﬀerence from the
presence of street trees.
The i-Tree Eco model calculated that the Drumcondra tree alley
could have a positive or negative in ﬂuence on air quality on an hourly
basis. The presence of trees can worsen air quality if deposited parti-
culate matter is resuspended into the atmosphere, which is more likely
to happen if there is high wind speed or been a long period of time
without rain, as rain removes particulate matter from leaf surfaces and
transfers it to the soil ( Nowak, Hirabayashi, Bodine, & Hoehn, 2013). At
wind speeds of 3 m/s, approximately 4.5 % of deposited particles have
been observed to be resuspended. This rate increases to 12 % re-
suspension at wind speeds of 9 m/s ( Nowak et al., 2013 ). Weather data
from Met Éireann (2019) indicates that Dublin experienced wind speeds
between 3.1 and 8.74 m/s when monitoring was taking place, which
would have been high enough to cause resuspension. However, PM 2.5

Liang, Ma, Wang, Wang, and Chen-xi (2016) documented that the
leaf morphology plays a positive factor for understanding the capture of
particulate matter. The study investigated the leaf hair, stomatal den-
sity, stomatal size, and the groove proportion and found that groove
proportion and the stomata size has direct e ﬀect in the removal of
PM2.5, whereas no positive correlation was found between PM 2.5
removal and leaf hairs or stomatal density ( Liang et al., 2016). How-
ever, Pääkkönen, Holopainen, and Kärenlampi (1997) noted that a high
amount of stomatal density and leaf thickness is e ﬀective in the removal
of several air pollutants, including gaseous pollutants. Jochner et al.
(2015) found that the leaf area density (LAD) and the thickness of trees
are e ﬀective in the reduction of NO x. The overall particulate matter
collection e ﬃciency increases with the presence of a cumulative leaf
area index and decreases with wind speed ( Huang et al., 2013).
With Dublin facing a 26 % increase in traﬃ c demand between 2011
and 2035, traﬃ c volume is only expected to increase across the city
(NTA, 2016 ) and lead to an associated increase in air pollution. Dublin
City Council (DCC) plants approximately 5000 trees in the city each
year (DCC, n.d.), so choosing to place them next to areas of high tra ﬃc
may help reduce pollutant concentrations in the immediate area. Al-
though Dublin is yet to breach the E.U. annual PM 2.5 limit value, taking
proactive measures, and developing e ﬀective policies to increase the
number of trees in Dublin may contribute to staying below this limit
despite future tra ﬃc increases.
5. Policy relevance
5.1. Tra ﬃc planning
Dublin’s tree canopy covers approximately 10 % of the city, with
most of the trees concentrated in South Dublin ( Brennan, Mills, &
Ningal, 2017 ). In June of 2018, Ireland’ s National Transport Agency
(NTA) announced the ‘BusConnects’ a plan for new primary bus routes
aimed at reducing tra ﬃc congestion within the urban centre ( O'Connor
and Caul ﬁeld, 2018; Baez, 2019 ). This proposed design requires
widening several of Dublin ’s busiest roads and removing between 1,500
and 4,700 street trees growing alongside them ( Burns, 2019; Mills,
2019). The BusConnects plan involves replacing the felled trees with
new saplings in alternate locations ( Burns, 2019), however, these young
trees would not have the same air ﬁltering ability as the original trees as
they would have a smaller surface area on which deposition would
occur. The BusConnects proposal has been met with some public re-
sistance from citizens concerned with the loss of trees. Residents from
Inichore, Dublin wrote to the NTA stating that saving the 150-year-old
trees in the area was their top priority and proposed an alternate route
through their neighbourhood that would leave tra ﬃc levels unchanged
(Roberts, 2019 ). The NTA later proposed a redesigned transport route
that would reduce the number of private car lanes but keep the mature
trees in place ( McGuire, 2019). A similar redesign was put forth for the
planned bus routes in Kimmage, Dublin ( Kelly, 2019). To date, much of
the resistance against removing trees has focused on their aesthetic
value and impact on property prices ( Brophy, 2018 ; Feehan, 2019),
however, increased awareness about the air- ﬁltering e ﬀects of trees
may in ﬂuence how residents respond to future traﬃ c route proposals

5.2. Public health policy
Public health is closely tied to environmental quality and air pol-
lution. Ireland ’s Department of Health (2019b) has identi ﬁed air pol-
lution as a climate scenario with profound health implications. This has
prompted it to introduce the ﬁrst Climate Change Adaptation Plan for
the Health Sector, developed under the National Adaptation Frame-
work 2018 and the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development Act
2015 ( Daly, 2019 ). Furthermore, the Irish Ministry for Health is es-
tablishing a Climate Change Oversight Group under the Department of
Health ( Department of Health, 2019a ). Air pollution is included in this
plan as climate change is expected to cause a weather-driven increase in
particulate matter and other pollutants ( Department of Health, 2019b ).
The main adaptation actions outlined in the plan are to increase air
quality monitoring, improve public education on the use of the EPA ’s
Air Quality Index, advocate for policies in all sectors to reduce emis-
sions, and create a research agenda aimed at better understanding the
health impacts of climate change ( Department of Health, 2019b). This
study can help inform the Climate Change Oversight Group as part of its
goal of promoting research on climate change adaptation measures. A
link between urban trees and air quality may also encourage the De-
partment of Health to advocate for policies that involve planting and
preserving trees in Dublin ( Constant, 2018 ).
5.3. Clean air policy
Ireland’s Department of Communications, Climate Action, and the
Environment (DCCAE) oversees implementing policy related to air
quality ( Broin, Kelly, Santos, Grythe, & Kelleher, 2019 ). The country ’s
ﬁrst national Clean Air Strategy (CAS) is set to be published in 2019,
along with the National Air Pollution Control Programme (NAPCP)
being developed in accordance with the EU National Emissions Ceilings
Directive ( DCCAE, 2019a , 2019b). The draft NAPCP sets emissions re-
duction targets and supporting measures to help achieve them, in-
cluding promoting low/non-smoke alternatives to solid domestic
heating fuel, transitioning public diesel buses to lower-emitting alter-
natives, and incentivizing the use of electric vehicles ( DCCAE, 2019c ).
The NAPCP and the CAS will have many overlapping elements and will
both cover policies relevant to the transport, energy, home heating, and
agriculture sectors ( DCCAE, 2019c ). The main pollutant-reducing
measures outlined in the draft NAPCP focus on preventing the emission
of pollutants at the source (Janhall, 2015). However, the air- ﬁltering
eﬀect of trees at the site could be relevant for clean air policy. With the
results of this study showing signi ﬁcantly lower PM 2.5 concentrations in
a tree-lined street compared to one without vegetation, incorporating
urban tree planting in Ireland’ s clean air strategies could help meet key
air quality targets.
6. Limitations
This study provides an exploratory assessment of the potential ef-
fects that street trees may have on improving air quality in Dublin.
Several limitations a ﬀect the conclusion of this study, mostly due to
constraints on time and equipment. Firstly, the study did not measure
or account for wind direction or speed. Since these factors are known to
inﬂuence air quality levels ( Amorim et al., 2013 ; Buccolieri et al., 2011;
Gromke & Ruck, 2012; Jin et al., 2014; Vos et al., 2013 ), this study
cannot conclusively determine how much of the air quality improve-
ment is due to the trees and how much is a result of di ﬀering air
currents. Furthermore, this study is limited by a relatively short air
monitoring period of ﬁve days. Extending the monitoring period across
several weeks or months would provide insight into any seasonal var-
iations in PM 2.5 levels. Expanding this study to include additional pairs
of ‘tree alley ’ and ‘non-tree’ monitoring locations would have also
supplied additional data with which to investigate the e ﬀect of trees.
Finally, collecting additional data on the tree alley (crown height, tree
height, leaf area index [LAI]) would have resulted in i-Tree Eco pro-
viding a more accurate estimate of the air quality improving capability
of the Drumcondra tree alley.
7. Conclusion
This study assesses the potential e ﬀect of urban trees on removing
particulate matter from the atmosphere using a novel combination of
air quality monitoring and the i-Tree Eco (UFORE) model. The objective
of this study is to investigate the potential of urban trees in removing
traﬃc-led and background air pollution through deposition and im-
proving the air quality in the built environment such as street canyon.
Monitoring was conducted in a tree alley and an area of no vegetation
along Drumcondra Road Lower in Dublin at a di ﬀerent time of day for
ﬁve days representing rush and non-rush hours. Measured air pollution
during the rush hour period can be considered to be generated from
traﬃc sources, while the pollution in the non-rush hour period re-
presents the locality’ s background air pollution. Results indicated that
there was no di ﬀerence in PM 2.5 concentrations outside of rush hours,
but concentrations were signi ﬁcantly lower in the tree alley during rush
hours when compared to the street section with no trees. The i-Tree Eco
model calculated that the 80 trees in the Drumcondra tree alley could
remove approximately 3 kg of PM 2.5 on a yearly basis and could result
in an increase or decrease in air quality depending on meteorological
and air quality conditions. Air monitoring found that the presence of
trees resulted in a maximum air quality improvement of 126 %, more
than double what was predicted by i-Tree Eco. This could be a result of
ad i ﬀerence in wind currents in both areas and out-of-date air quality
data used by the model. While this study provides an initial exploration
of trees and air quality in Dublin, further research comparing additional
pairs of tree alleys and street sections with no trees across di ﬀerent
seasons would allow for a better understanding of how Dublin ’s trees
can aﬀect its air quality. The preliminary results from this study suggest
that they do cause a degree of air quality improvement and can be used
to inform several national clean air strategies aimed at reducing pol-
lutant emissions. Widening the scope of Ireland ’s air quality policies to
include urban greenery management could be an additional measure to
help reach future emissions targets.
