 A B S T R A C T
Data-driven surrogate models provide an effective means to reduce the high computational cost of compu-
tational fluid dynamics (CFD), enabling large-scale design and uncertainty studies that would otherwise be 
infeasible. Most existing surrogate models for CFD of pollution dispersion focus primarily on variations in 
boundary conditions, while other uncertain factors are rarely considered. We extend the input space beyond 
boundary conditions to include both modeling uncertainties (turbulence parameters) and key physical drivers 
such as emission strength, source characteristics, inlet profiles, and solar irradiance, creating a database 
with nearly 50000 converged samples. We emulate the two-dimensional RANS simulations of the canonical 
thermal street-canyon problem in a simplified grid of 64 × 64 points within the canyon. We benchmark two 
surrogate modeling approaches, namely Principal Component Analysis combined with a multilayer perceptron 
(PCA+MLP) and a Dense layer Convolutional Encoder–Decoder with skipped connections (DCED) surrogate 
model to contrast linear and nonlinear dimensionality reduction strategies. To assess performance, we introduce 
a combined regression–classification error framework based on vortex-direction prediction. PCA+MLP is 
computationally efficient, while DCED delivers slightly higher accuracy at larger training and inference 
costs. The PCA+MLP model attains a regression error of 4.42% and a classification error of 2.24%, while 
providing an inference speed-up of approximately five orders of magnitude relative to a single iteration of 
the CFD model. The DCED achieves slightly lower errors (3.89% regression and 2.20% classification), with 
an inference acceleration of about three orders of magnitude. For both models, performance decreases in 
thermally dominated regimes, highlighting the challenge of capturing strongly nonlinear flow phenomena. 
Our results also emphasize the importance of dataset balance and suggest targeted sampling strategies for 
better generalization. We publicly release our CFD benchmark database to support reproducibility and future 
research.
1. Introduction
Current urbanization trends project an increase in global urban 
population from 55% in 2025 to 68% by 2050. Consequently, the 
development of livable cities that can accommodate this growth in a 
sustainable manner is becoming increasingly critical. Among the key 
challenges, urban air quality is a major concern. The WHO estimates 
that 91% of the urban population is exposed to levels of pollution that 
exceeded the recommended thresholds [1]. In this context, the ability 
to assess, monitor and predict urban air quality is emerging as a vital 
tool for informed urban planning and public health protection.
Although monitoring stations are key for assessing urban air quality, 
they offer limited spatial representativeness [2]. In contrast, numerical

surrogate models are increasingly being investigated as efficient al-
ternatives, as they can approximate CFD outputs at a fraction of the 
time. Such models enable large-scale tasks in urban flow simulations 
that require repeated evaluations, including optimization [12,13], sen-
sitivity analysis [14,15], and uncertainty quantification [16,17]. These 
applications often rely on numerical derivatives, which may be difficult 
to estimate accurately in practice. While the differentiability of neural 
networks does not formally guarantee the fidelity of their derivatives 
with respect to the true governing equations, surrogate models remain 
a practical tool for repeated evaluations, provided that derivative-based 
analyses are performed and interpreted with appropriate care.
Surrogate models can be classified as intrusive or non-intrusive. 
Intrusive models (often referred to as intrusive reduced-order models) 
are derived by algebraically reducing the governing equations [18,19], 
but they may suffer from stability issues and inefficiencies when dealing 
with nonlinear systems. Non-intrusive models, in contrast, rely on data-
driven strategies to map inputs to outputs and do not require any 
modification of the code. In this study, we focus on non-intrusive, 
data-driven surrogate modeling.
Classical non-intrusive surrogate models typically employ linear 
dimensionality reduction, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), 
to extract the dominant modes of the flow field, followed by re-
gression models to predict modal amplitudes as a function of input 
parameters [16,20–23]. Such PCA+regression models are often more 
interpretable and require less training data, but can struggle with 
strongly nonlinear flows [24,25]. Different regression models have been 
deployed to estimate the modes, such as polynomial chaos expan-
sion [26], kriging [27,28] and deep-belief networks [29]. Multilayer 
perceptrons (MLPs) offer a flexible, non-intrusive alternative that can 
approximate complex nonlinear mappings between input parameters 
and PCA coefficients while remaining relatively simple to train and fast 
to deploy.
More recently, non-intrusive surrogate modeling strategies have 
increasingly leveraged deep learning to capture nonlinear structure in 
high-dimensional data. Approaches based on generative models such 
as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [30] and operator learn-
ing architectures such as the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) [31–34] 
have demonstrated strong potential for representing complex tran-
sient dynamics and accommodating variable geometries. While these 
methods are not dimensionality reduction techniques in the classical 
sense, they implicitly learn nonlinear latent representations that enable 
efficient surrogate construction. In parallel, well-established nonlin-
ear dimensionality reduction techniques such as autoencoders [35] 
have been widely adopted. Autoencoders consist of an encoder that

However, this increased representational power typically comes at the

In particular, most surrogate models for urban CFD simulations have 
primarily focused on geometric input variables [30,37–39] or boundary 
conditions [21,23,24,30,40–42], while other uncertain factors influenc-
ing pollution fields are often neglected. This study addresses this gap by 
comparing PCA+regression and autoencoder-based non-intrusive surro-
gate models across a broad set of input variables on two-dimensional 
RANS simulations of the heated street-canyon case. The thermal street 
canyon problem is a very common case in pollution dispersion and 
features a significant number of parameters, making it suitable for this 
study.
With the exception of geometry, we incorporate nearly all pa-
rameters identified a priori as influential, including turbulence model 
settings, inlet concentration profiles, emission rates, source height and 
heat release, inlet velocity profiles, and solar irradiance, resulting in 
a total of 18 parameters. By perturbing these parameters, we gener-
ate a database of approximately 50000 converged RANS simulations, 
with each simulation stored on a 64 × 64 grid. In line with previ-
ous work [43], we propose a surrogate modeling framework based 
on nonlinear dimensionality reduction, in which scalar input param-
eters are first mapped to a high-dimensional image representation 
through a fully connected (dense) layer and reshaped onto a spatial 
grid. This representation is subsequently processed by a convolutional 
encoder–decoder architecture with skip connections, enabling efficient 
learning of complex nonlinear mappings between the input param-
eters and high-dimensional output fields. We refer to this architec-
ture as Dense Convolutional Encoder–Decoder (DCED) to highlight 
the initial dense layer that bridges the low-dimensional parameter 
space and the high-dimensional spatial representation. We benchmark 
PCA combined with a multilayer perceptron (PCA+MLP) and DCED 
approaches, highlighting trade-offs between linear and nonlinear di-
mensionality reduction strategies. For both surrogate strategies, the 
model architecture and training hyperparameters are automatically op-
timized using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-
II), which balances accuracy, model complexity, and data efficiency. To 
assess performance, we introduce a combined regression–classification 
error framework based on vortex-direction prediction, providing a 
physics-informed evaluation and illustrating the challenges of capturing 
highly nonlinear flow patterns under natural convection. We made 
publicly available the full RANS database and the surrogate models 
to support reproducibility and further research in data-driven urban 
environmental assessment.
1.1. Research contributions
The main contributions of this study are the following:
• Expanded parametric space: this space incorporates 18 distinct 
input variables, capturing interactions among turbulence model 
uncertainties, variable pollution source characteristics, inlet pro-
files of velocity and concentration, and solar irradiance.
• Methodological benchmarking: a comparative assessment of 
linear (PCA+MLP) versus nonlinear (DCED) dimensionality re-
duction strategies, supported by a multi-objective genetic algo-
rithm optimization process.
• Physics-informed framework: Implementation of a dual regres
sion-classification error framework that utilizes vortex-direction 
prediction and a total pollutant mass metric to evaluate model 
performance under complex flow regimes. This approach reveals 
limitations of surrogate models in reproducing highly nonlinear, 
thermally dominated flow regimes.
• Open-access resources: to facilitate reproducibility and future 
data-driven environmental research, we provide a large RANS 
database of nearly 50,000 converged simulations of the canon-
ical thermal street-canyon problem. Furthermore, the optimized 
surrogate models are made publicly available to serve as a bench-
mark for future architectural developments.
2. Methods
To advance CFD urban pollution surrogate modeling, we consider 
a wide input parametric space including 18 parameters. We first build 
a large RANS database for the canonical street canyon case (𝑁𝑠𝑎𝑚𝑝𝑙𝑒𝑠 =
49590), systematically perturbing these input parameters to capture 
their influence in the resulting pollution field. The dataset is randomly 
divided into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) subsets. 
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the training set is used to fit the surrogates, 
the validation set for model architecture optimization with NSGA-II, 
and the test set for comparing the surrogate model strategies. We have 
chosen to benchmark PCA+MLP and DCED non-intrusive surrogate 
models approaches to contrast linear versus nonlinear dimensionality 
reduction strategies.
2.1. Ground truth database construction: simulation of pollution dispersion 
within a street canyon
In this subsection, we begin by presenting the physical model 
studied, its mathematical formulation, and the numerical solution strat-
egy. Next, we discuss the perturbed input parameters and their se-
lected ranges to build the database, which is publicly available on 
Zenodo [44].
2.1.1. Street canyon physical model
We study the canonical two-dimensional street canyon shown in 
Fig. 2. The canyon domain represents the region close to ground level 
bounded by buildings, where vehicle exhaust pollution is prone to 
accumulate due to air recirculation flow patterns. A two-dimensional 
canyon implies a direction of wind orthogonal to the street, which 
constitutes the least favorable configuration for ventilation. Despite 
its simplicity in geometry, the resulting pollution distribution is quite 
complex, encompassing several phenomena such as flow separation, 
local recirculation regions, and thermal effects [45]. We consider two 
square buildings of height 𝐻, separated by a street of width 𝐿, in 
our case 𝐻 = 𝐿 = 20 m. Following previous work [5,8,46,47], we 
consider a single pollutant source located in the center of the canyon. 
We parameterize the height of the release (𝑄ℎ), the emission mass rate 
(𝑄𝑐 ), and the heat release rate (𝑄𝑝) to account for the hot exhaust gases 
produced by internal combustion vehicles.
As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), a primary clockwise-rotating vortex typi-
cally forms within the canyon as a result of forced convection. How-
ever, as shown in Fig. 3(b), this vortex can be modified or even 
reversed due to the influence of natural convection. To evaluate the 
ability of the surrogate model to capture these flow patterns, we define 
two categories: clockwise and counterclockwise rotating vortices. This 
classification is based on a comparison of the mean spatial pollutant 
concentration between the left half of the canyon (𝛺𝑙𝑒𝑓 𝑡) and the

2.1.5. Distributions of input parameters
A large database of pollutant concentration fields is generated by 
systematically perturbing boundary conditions and model parameters. 
With the exception of geometric characteristics, we include nearly all 
input parameters considered a priori to be influential. Geometric vari-
ations are not considered in this study, as modifying the canyon width 
or building heights would require generating a new mesh for each 
configuration, substantially increasing both computational cost and 
the dimensionality of the parameter space. A canonical street-canyon 
geometry is therefore adopted to isolate the effects of turbulence model 
parameters together with kinematic, background concentration, ther-
mal and pollutant emission boundary conditions. The complete set of 
18 input parameters (𝑁𝑖 = 18 ) is summarized in Table 1, where they 
are classified into five distinct groups:
1. RANS group contains all parameters related to the turbulence mod-
eling. The range for 𝑆𝑐𝑡 is based on a previous sensitivity study [63] 
on the effect of 𝑆𝑐𝑡 on mass dispersion in a street canyon. All 
other distributions of RANS parameters are from a previous Bayesian 
calibration analysis in a street canyon [64].
2. Inlet kinematics group defines the inlet profiles of velocity, turbu-
lence intensity, and turbulence dissipation. The friction velocity, 𝑈𝜏 , 
is chosen to cover a wide range of wind speed scenarios. However, a 
too low inlet speed compromises convergence; therefore, we define 
a low speed limit to ensure a reasonable convergence rate. The 
aerodynamic roughness length range, 𝑦0, is based on a previous 
analysis [65] that reported values from 0.52 in low-mean building 
height up to 2.59 in high-rise urban areas. Whereas previous studies 
have assumed unit values for 𝑝𝑘 and 𝑝𝜖 factors [58] from Eq. (10), 
we introduce uncertainty by modeling each factor with a lognormal 
distribution that has a median of 1 and a log scale standard devi-
ation of 0.4. This yields a 95% confidence interval that stretches 
approximately [0.45, 2.23].
3. Background Concentration group defines the inlet concentration 
profile as defined in Eq. (11). The background concentration con-
stant, 𝐵𝑘, is based on previously reported values [66]. The linear 
factor, 𝑝𝐵, has negative and positive values to account for the 
different vertical stratification scenarios.
4. Emission group defines the single source of black carbon pollution 
located in the center of the canyon. The upper limit of 𝑄𝑐 is based 
on data collected in [67]. The range of source height, 𝑄ℎ, is defined 
to span a representative range of vehicle geometries, with its limit 
values adjusted to align with the points of the grid. The associated 
heat release, 𝑄𝑝, is estimated based on the reported exhaustion 
temperature and mass flux [68,69].
5. Thermal group defines the angle of solar incidence, 𝜃, covering 
the entire range from 0◦ to 180◦, and the temperature increase, 
𝛥𝑇 , which is estimated based on experimental data from [70]. 
Their study showed that sun-exposed concrete walls can experience 
temperature increases of up to 30 K.
To avoid redundant sampling by covering the parametric space more 
uniformly, while preserving randomness properties, we use a Quasi-
Monte Carlo (QMC) sampling method based on a low-discrepancy Sobol 
sequence [71]. Specifically, we generate 18-dimensional parameter 
combinations using the Python library SciPy [72].

2.2.2. Dense layer Convolutional Encoder–Decoder with skipped connec-
tions (DCED)
Unlike the PCA+MLP approach, which reduces the dimensionality 
of the physical pollution fields by a linear combination of principal 
components before feeding the MLP, the DCED architecture operates 
directly on high-dimensional structured data. DCED leverages a con-

features. The encoder compresses the input via convolution and pooling 
layers to capture hierarchical patterns, while the decoder restores 
resolution through upsampling. Skip connections link encoder and de-
coder stages at corresponding levels, potentially preserving fine-grained 
spatial details lost during downsampling. These nonlinear processes 
suggest that DCED may perform better compared to the more compact 
linear PCA + regression model pipelines.

2.2.3. Hyperparameter optimization using non-dominated sorting genetic 
algorithm
The MLP and DCED hyperparameters defined in Tables 2 and 3 
are optimized using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II 
(NSGA-II) [78], a widely adopted multiobjective evolutionary algo-
rithm [79,80]. Like other genetic algorithms (GAs), NSGA-II evolves a 
population of candidate solutions through iterative processes of selec-
tion, crossover, and mutation, mimicking natural selection. However, it

extends traditional GAs by incorporating a non-dominated sorting ap-
proach and an elitist strategy to handle multiple conflicting objectives 
efficiently.
This method is used to tune the training configurations for each 
network, resulting in optimized models whose hyperparameters are 
jointly adjusted to satisfy four competing objectives:
1. The regression error (𝜉) is computed as the normalized mean 
square error defined in Eq. (15). To ensure the overall predictive 
capacity in the population, a restriction on regression errors is set 
to 10%, that is, samples with a regression error above 10% will 
automatically be considered as non-dominant. This value was set 
after preliminary tests and ensures relatively high-quality networks 
while allowing exploration of the parametric space.
2. To evaluate the surrogate model’s ability to predict changes in flow 
patterns, we also consider a binary classification error rate (𝛾) 
which is defined as the proportion of misclassified samples: 
𝛾 = 𝐹 𝑁 + 𝐹 𝑃𝐹 𝑁 + 𝐹 𝑃 + 𝑇 𝑁 + 𝑇 𝑃 , (17)
where 𝐹 𝑁 (False Negatives) and 𝐹 𝑃 (False Positives) correspond 
to incorrectly classified samples, while 𝑇 𝑁 (True Negatives) and 
𝑇 𝑃 (True Positives) represent correctly classified ones. A sample 
is considered positive if a counterclockwise vortex is predicted, and
negative otherwise.
3. The training dataset size refers to the number of random sam-
ples drawn from the total 𝑁train samples available for training. 
Reducing the size of the training dataset, while preserving learning 
efficiency, is essential for lowering the substantial computational 
cost associated with generating CFD databases.
4. The size of the neural network, quantified by the number of 
trainable parameters, affects its generalization ability, computa-
tional cost, and susceptibility to overfitting. It is therefore essential 
to minimize network size while preserving sufficient capacity to 
accurately model the underlying physical phenomena.
NSGA-II identifies a diverse set of non-dominated solutions, i.e.,
those that are optimal in at least one objective given a set of all others. 
To maintain population diversity and convergence stability, NSGA-II 
employs a crowding distance mechanism and a (𝛽+𝜙) selection strategy, 
where 𝛽 represents the parent population and 𝜙 the offspring. This 
elitist approach ensures that the best solutions are preserved over gen-
erations. The resulting Pareto front provides a set of optimal trade-offs, 
allowing for informed selection of model configurations that balance 
accuracy, complexity, and data efficiency. Our NSGA-II implementation 
is based on the Pyhton library Pymoo [81].

Due to its architectural design, the DCED has a network size approx-

This increased complexity leads to significantly higher computational 
costs during training. However, PCA+MLP appears to require larger 
training datasets to achieve regression errors comparable to those of 
DCED. The opposite trend is observed for the classification error.
Analysis of the candidate architectures generated during the NSGA-
II search reveals consistent trends in network performance. For the 
PCA+MLP models, relatively shallow architectures (2–4 layers) consis-
tently outperform deeper configurations, which appear to be affected 
by vanishing gradient effects. The best-performing four-layer networks 
exhibit an increase–decrease–increase neuron pattern, suggesting the 
presence of a bottleneck that promotes compact feature extraction. 
Early stopping acted as the primary regularization mechanism, and 
additional techniques such as dropout or weight decay did not yield 
measurable improvements. For DCED models, performance was pri-
marily influenced by the number of filters in the initial convolutional 
layers, with higher entry-layer filter counts generally leading to better 
predictive accuracy. While the NSGA-II search successfully identified 
several high-performing architecture configurations, the computational 
cost of evaluating many candidate networks per generation makes this 
approach impractical for training large ensembles of surrogate models.

4. Conclusion
This study developed and evaluated non-intrusive surrogate models 
for pollution dispersion in a thermal street canyon. A comprehensive 
two-dimensional RANS database was generated, covering key physi-
cal and modeling parameters such as turbulence settings, inlet pro-
files, emissions, source height, heat release, and solar irradiance. We 
benchmarked a PCA+MLP approach against a convolution encoder–
decoder architecture, with model architectures and training hyperpa-
rameters optimized via NSGA-II to balance accuracy, complexity, and 
data efficiency. Performance was assessed using a combined regression–
classification framework capturing both field predictions and vortex 
orientation.
The analysis indicates that both surrogate approaches, PCA+MLP 
and DCED, exhibit similar performance in terms of regression and 
classification errors. The PCA+MLP approach is considerably more 
computationally efficient during training, whereas DCED demonstrates 
slightly better performance in terms of regression accuracy.
Despite the simplicity of two-dimensional street-canyon geometry, 
performance decreases when thermal effects dominate and the main 
vortex is inverted. This is mainly due to the highly nonlinear response 
to input parameters in this regime and the limited number of samples 
available to fully capture such patterns. In this context, the current 
database could be further extended by heavily sampling the thermally 
dominated regime, as described by the input parameter ranges in 
Section 3.1, which primarily correspond to strong heating of the ground 
and the inner wall of the downwind building.
Surrogate models could be further improved using more complex ar-
chitectures, such as generative adversarial neural networks (GANs) and 
physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). We make our dataset and 
surrogate modeling efforts publicly available in the hope of fostering 
the development of emulators for complex thermal flows.
This work highlights the potential and limitations of data-driven 
surrogate models as computationally efficient alternatives to traditional 
CFD simulations for urban environmental assessments. The findings 
emphasize the importance of carefully handling nonlinear regimes, par-
