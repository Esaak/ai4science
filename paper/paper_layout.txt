                                               Abstract
     Reliable, spatially resolved prediction of pollutant concentration in an urban canopy is expensive
with Large Eddy Simulation (LES) and out of reach for Gaussian models that ignore the geometry.
We train a convolutional surrogate neural model that maps a raster description of an idealized, regular
staggered-block, urban canopy described in building geometry, leaf-area index, road emission sources,
and wind — onto the steady-state PM2.5 field at four heights and its vertical mean, using data from
the MSU-RCC LES model. On the test set the surrogate reaches 𝑅2 = 0.86 with FAC2 = 0.75,
NMSE = 0.44 and low bias (FB = 0.07, mass_ratio = 0.93), satisfying the Chang–Hanna acceptance
criteria. We demonstrate that the predicted field is governed primarily by the emission sources and
the vegetation. Also, we demonstrate that the feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) on the wind
vector, intended to improve the fit, gives no measurable benefit in a controlled ablation study and
slightly worsens the mass-balance metric. By contrast, data augmentation is shown to be essential.
We also demonstrate the computational efficiency: the surrogate model runs in about 3.5 ms per city
configuration.

1.     INTRODUCTION


      Air quality is a significant factor of public health, and road traffic is one of the leading con­
tributors to urban air pollution [1]. A trustworthy spatial estimate of the pollutant distribution
in a city requires resolving the interaction of the wind flow with the building geometry, including
street-canyon channelling and recirculation in the wakes of buildings.
      Numerical approaches to turbulent scalar transport trade accuracy against computational
cost, with the two well-established families occupying opposite extremes of this trade-off. Large
Eddy Simulation (LES) resolves the energy-containing eddies responsible for most of the scalar
transport and is the reference tool for urban-canopy flows [2, 3], but reaching a quasi-stationary
regime takes wall-clock time that precludes operational analysis or a sweep over many config­
urations. At the opposite side, semi-empirical Gaussian plume models are cheap but do not

account for the building geometry at all. Machine-learning surrogates trained on high-fidelity
data can approximate the non-linear map from boundary conditions to the dispersion field at
a cost comparable to analytical models: neural networks have been used to emulate urban
pollutant dispersion for emergency forecasting [4] and, more generally, convolutional networks
predict steady flow fields orders of magnitude faster than a Computational Fluid Dynamics
(CFD) solver [5].
     Machine-learning surrogates for airflow and dispersion in the built environment have devel­
oped rapidly; a comprehensive review is given by Calzolari and Liu [6]. Coupled CFD–ML
models estimate ventilation and pollutant exchange in dense urban settings [7], dedicated emu­
lators target operational dispersion forecasting for accidental releases [4], and operator-learning
methods such as the Fourier neural operator approximate the solution map of the governing
equations directly [8]. Most of these surrogates, however, predict a single near-surface plane
rather than the vertical structure of the field, fix the emission sources and vary only the inflow,
and report aggregate error without the physically grounded metrics standard in air-quality
model evaluation.
     This work develops a fast surrogate for the steady-state PM2.5 field of an idealized urban
canopy at several heights and analyses which physical inputs govern its predictions. It differs
from prior machine-learning surrogates in three respects: the target is multi-level (four heights
and the vertical mean) rather than a single near-surface plane; the model is evaluated with
the standard Chang–Hanna dispersion criteria and an explicit mass-balance check, not with
𝑅2 alone; and a controlled component ablation study quantifies the roles of wind conditioning
and data augmentation. Two results are established. First, for the present setup the predicted
field is governed primarily by the emission sources and the vegetation. Second, the ablation
study establishes that data augmentation is essential to accuracy, whereas the wind-conditioning
mechanism (Feature-wise Linear Modulation, FiLM) yields no measurable improvement and
slightly worsens the mass-balance metric.



2.    DATA


     2.1.   Physical problem statement


     The reference data are generated with LES of an idealized urban canopy under neutral
thermal stratification, representative of Moscow conditions (Fig. 1). The domain is 256 ×
256 × 64 m, discretised on a 128 × 128 × 32 grid (2 m per cell in the horizontal). The building

                                               x–3
                                Deep Learning in Computational Physics


geometry is fixed across the whole dataset: identical blocks of height 16 m in a regular staggered
array occupying about 6% of the plan area. Vegetation is represented by trees with prescribed
leaf-area density (LAD) and leaf-area index (LAI); roads are modelled as volumetric PM2.5
emission sources of height 8 m. The flow is forced by a prescribed horizontal pressure gradient
that sets the wind; the wind speed is essentially constant across the dataset (varying by <0.5%),
so only the wind direction and the emission and vegetation layout vary between simulations.

   2.3.   Input representation and dataset


   Each LES run reaches a quasi-stationary state, after which the concentration field is time-av­
eraged to give the target. The dataset comprises 9 layouts and 2670 simulations (“experiments”),
each carrying several independently released tracers, for a total of 58566 samples. The input
for each tracer is a 7 × 128 × 128 raster (Fig. 2): normalised building height; a signed-distance
function (SDF) map to buildings; the leaf-area index (LAI); the road emission power (below);
a signed-distance map to roads (source SDF); and two constant planes encoding the wind di­
rection (cos 𝜑, sin 𝜑). A signed-distance encoding of geometry is a standard, effective input for
convolutional flow surrogates [5]. Four normalised coordinate planes (coordinate convolution,
CoordConv [15]) are appended inside the network, and the full wind 3-vector (cos 𝜑, sin 𝜑, |v|) is
also supplied to the wind-conditioning module (Sec. 3.2). The prediction target has five maps:
the concentration at 𝑧 = 3, 10, 16, 50 m (levels Z1, Z5, Z8, Z25) and the vertically averaged
concentration (“Mean”). All concentrations are transformed by log(1 + 𝐶) for training, as they
span several orders of magnitude.

   The road-emission power is reconstructed from the rasterised road mask: connected segments
are split into length ℓ and width 𝑤 by morphological opening and assigned a strength ℓ 𝑤2 ℎ𝑠 𝑘𝑒
(ℎ𝑠 = 8 m, 𝑘𝑒 = 13.06/3.5), passed to the network as log(1 + power). Figure 3 summarises the
dataset: the mean concentration of the central level, the vegetated area fraction, the log10 of the
peak source power (bimodal, spanning about two orders of magnitude), and the distribution of
wind directions.

   The split into training and test sets is by experiment (80/20, fixed seed): all tracers of
one simulation stay on the same side, giving 46934 training and 11632 test samples. Because
the geometry is identical everywhere, the layouts are pooled before splitting; the consequences
for generalisation are discussed in Sec. 5. Spatial augmentation (90∘ rotations and reflections,
applied consistently to the raster, the target and the wind vector) is used on the training set
only.

3.    METHOD


     3.1.   Network architecture


     The surrogate is a U-Net [16] with about 5.1×106 parameters (Fig. 4). The encoder is a stem
convolution to 64 channels followed by three max-pooling stages to 128, 256 and 512 channels

                                                  x–6
                      Moscow University Physics Bulletin 80(9), x (2026)


(Mish activations). The decoder has three stages, each a transposed convolution, concatenation
of the encoder skip connection, and a modulated double convolution (convolution, GroupNorm,
wind modulation, Mish, convolution, GroupNorm, Mish) with 256, 128 and 64 channels. A 1×1
convolution produces the five output maps. The output is masked geometrically: at each height
the prediction is zeroed in cells where the building is taller than that level (the “Mean” map is
left unmasked); the same masking is applied in the metrics so that trivial zeros inside buildings
do not inflate the scores.




Figure 4. Architecture: a U-Net with a 64–128–256–512 encoder and skip connections. The global
wind vector is embedded by an MLP and injected into every decoder level through FiLM (𝛾, 𝛽).




   3.2.   Wind conditioning via FiLM


   To test whether an explicit, strict dependence on wind improves the fit, the decoder is
conditioned on the global wind vector through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) [17].
The wind 3-vector is embedded by a two-layer MLP (3 → 128 → 512) and, at each decoder
level ℓ, a linear projection yields a per-channel scale 𝛾ℓ and shift 𝛽ℓ ; the feature map after the
first convolution and normalisation is modulated as

                                 FiLM(hℓ ) = (1 + 𝛾ℓ ) ⊙ hℓ + 𝛽ℓ .

Disabling the conditioning (“no FiLM”, used in the ablation study) forces 𝛾ℓ = 𝛽ℓ = 0. Whether
this mechanism actually helps is tested in Sec. 4.3.

   3.5.   Baselines and training


   Two geometry-agnostic baselines are used: a mean-field baseline (the training-set average
field, per pixel and level) and a nearest-wind baseline (the target of the training sample whose
wind vector is closest). Models are trained with AdamW (learning rate 10−5 , batch size 256)
and a ReduceLROnPlateau schedule (factor 0.5, patience 5); each epoch takes about 200 s. Two
checkpoints are kept, one at minimum validation loss and one at maximum validation 𝑅2 ; the
plume-weighted loss and 𝑅2 anti-correlate late in training, so the reported production model is
the best-𝑅2 checkpoint, obtained by fine-tuning a converged model for a further 100 epochs.

     4.2.   Baselines


     Both geometry-agnostic baselines have negative 𝑅2 and FAC2 < 0.2 (mean-field 𝑅2 = −0.31,
FAC2 = 0.19; nearest-wind 𝑅2 = −0.72, FAC2 = 0.15), i.e. they are worse than predicting the
mean, so the surrogate’s skill comes from resolving the source and vegetation layout, not from
the marginal statistics of the field or from the wind alone.

the production number and the two should not be compared directly. We can not claim the
production model would be worse without FiLM.

   4.4.   Governing inputs


   For the problem setup considered here, the predicted field is determined chiefly by the emis­
sion input — the location and magnitude of the sources — and by the vegetation (LAI), which
redistributes and removes the scalar; the fixed building geometry and the single-speed wind
direction play a secondary role. This ordering is consistent with the ablation study of Sec. 4.3,
in which removing the explicit wind conditioning leaves accuracy essentially unchanged, and
with the dataset design, in which the source and vegetation layouts are the primary degrees
of freedom. We emphasise that it is specific to the present regime (fixed geometry, fixed wind
speed) and is not a general statement about the relative importance of these inputs.

   4.5.   Physical validity


   Across three different test scenes, the predicted fields place the plume correctly but smear
its fine structure (Fig. 5), and the parity plot (Fig. 6) shows a background floor near 𝐶 ≈ 0 and
under-prediction of the highest concentrations — the signature of a log-space MSE objective.
Mass is well calibrated in the median (Fig. 7); a small subset of scenes (1.5% of the test set,
with mass_ratio < 0.1) forms a separate cluster near mass_ratio ≈ 0.05 and FB ≈ 1.6, i.e. the
surrogate predicts a near-zero field where the LES contains a strong plume.

   4.6.   Computational cost


   The surrogate evaluates one scene in about 3.5 ms on an NVIDIA Tesla V100-DGXS-32GB
GPU (∼284 scenes/s). For reference, one LES run on the same GPU takes about 20 min for a
single-tracer configuration and about 30 min when 16 tracers are released simultaneously, the
flow solve dominating the cost. Per delivered concentration field this is ≈ 1.2 × 103 s (single
tracer) or ≈ 1.1 × 102 s (amortised over 16 tracers), so the surrogate is faster by four to five

5.    DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS


     Three limitations shape the interpretation. First, the objective is the main weakness: it is
a log-space error, whereas the quantities of interest (mass, bias) are physical. The features the
model handles worst — peak magnitude and physical mass, and the very quantities in which the
ablation variants differ — are exactly those the log loss does not constrain. Rebuilding the loss
on a physical concentration normalisation (a mass-consistent term, or training partly in physical
space) is the natural next step. Second, the geometry and wind speed are fixed: generalisation
to unseen building morphologies or wind speeds is out of scope, and the marginal role of the
wind conditioning is a property of this regime, not a general statement about FiLM. Because
the layouts share a common geometry, the test set does not probe geometric generalisation;
the meaningful axis here would be held-out source and vegetation configurations, which are the
dominant predictors. Third, the emission-power channel is reconstructed from a raster and is
fragile for non-axis-aligned roads.

     Finally, we note the practical finding that a plume-weighted loss and 𝑅2 anti-correlate late
in training, so checkpoint selection must be stated explicitly.




6.    CONCLUSION


     In this study, we presented the fast convolutional surrogate neural model for the steady-state
PM2.5 field of an idealized urban canopy that satisfies standard dispersion-model acceptance cri­
teria (𝑅2 = 0.86, FAC2 = 0.75, NMSE = 0.44). We demonstrate that the predicted PM2.5 field
is governed primarily by the emission sources and vegetation configuration. Also, a controlled
ablation study shows that data augmentation is essential whereas explicit wind conditioning
(FiLM) gives no measurable benefit in prediction quality and slightly worsens the mass-balance
metric. We intend to explore the alternative options for loss formulations since we see the
log-space loss being the main limitation in the current problem formulation.
